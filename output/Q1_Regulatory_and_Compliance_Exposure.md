# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Regulatory regimes and supervisory priorities

- Meridian is authorised and regulated by the FCA and PRA in the UK, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. In FY2025, it engaged with supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness and consumer outcomes. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Meridian's regulated subsidiaries are located in Dublin (Meridian Financial Services (Ireland) DAC), Luxembourg (Meridian Fund Services S.A.), Singapore (Meridian Asia Pte Ltd) and Hong Kong (Meridian Capital Markets (HK) Limited), implying multi-jurisdictional regulatory exposure. [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- Management states that the pace of regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk and AI governance) places a material demand on second- and third-line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- The regulator expects customer due diligence (CDD), enhanced due diligence (EDD) for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice, with emphasis on periodic tuning and testing and on financial crime resourcing. Control capability must be scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Firms must identify important business services, set impact tolerances, map supporting resources (including third parties) and complete scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most commonly incomplete element and is treated as a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to maintain a complete register of material outsourcing, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products that are no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Firms deploying AI or generative AI should maintain an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, assess consumer and conduct implications, and not scale deployment ahead of governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Emerging AI requirements converge on the following: an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. AI used in onboarding, credit or advice attracts the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Digital operational resilience requirements extend to critical ICT providers, covering contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party contractual arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- There is increasing scrutiny of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in training and operating AI systems. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- Supervisors are moving toward demonstrable effectiveness testing in preference to framework documentation, and expect independent validation of control performance and of remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend into legacy and closed-book products, with a heightened expectation that firms identify affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

### Financial crime remediation status and the conflicting evidence

- Board minutes record the programme as 'substantially complete': all twelve high-priority supervisory review findings have been remediated and closed, with closure evidence accepted by second line. Three medium-priority findings remain open and are due for closure in the current quarter; no high-priority findings are open. The Board asked for formal programme closure, with remaining items moved to business-as-usual (BAU). This conflicts with the external press report's claim that four findings remain open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- According to sources familiar with the supervisory dialogue, supervisors do not regard the financial crime remediation programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- This differs materially from Q3 2025 board reporting, which described the programme as substantially complete with all high-priority findings closed. A person close to the supervisor said some findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation because closure evidence was insufficient. Meridian declined to comment on supervisory correspondence and told investors the agenda was 'well advanced and moving to completion'. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Management states that substantial progress has been made across the financial crime remediation portfolio, but that certain workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year. This is more cautious than the Q3 2025 board minutes' 'substantially complete' and is consistent with the press report of open findings. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- The plan includes closure validation of the financial crime remediation programme, implying that independent validation of closure has not yet been completed. This bears on whether remediation is truly complete. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The client considered independent validation of financial crime remediation closure but deferred it pending completion of the programme. This means no independent validation of remediation closure was commissioned, which bears on the conflict between the board minutes and the press reporting over whether remediation is complete. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Closure of remediation findings was in several cases evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. This is relevant to assessing claims that Meridian's remediation is substantially complete. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- The peer reported to its board that a remediation programme was complete, but independent testing found that a number of findings had been closed without adequate evidence. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor identified inaccurate internal reporting to the board as the most serious aggravating factor, which delayed corrective action by about eighteen months. It stated that boards must satisfy themselves on the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Analysts noted that the discrepancy, if confirmed, would echo a recent peer enforcement action in which inaccurate internal board reporting was an aggravating factor. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Protiviti treats closure validation as a distinct assurance activity: evidence of closure is tested independently against the original finding, and a finding is regarded as closed only where the underlying control is demonstrated to operate effectively, not on the basis of management assertion. This is relevant to assessing management claims that financial crime remediation is complete. [PV-04, Closure Validation Principle]
- Findings are rated by risk and their root causes identified; actions are agreed with defined owners and dates and reported to the accountable committee. Closure of actions is validated through independent evidence testing rather than management assertion. [PV-04, Phase 5 — Reporting and Follow-Up]
- The Board approved an increase in the onboarding volume appetite threshold in Institutional Services on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and assessed as effective. The decision therefore rests on the contested remediation claim. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at the client's request and carried forward, leaving a sanctions screening effectiveness area without audit coverage in FY2024. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]

### Internal control gaps, risk-governance weaknesses and open findings

- The FY2025 effectiveness review identified three gaps: inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and the absence of a defined framework for AI governance. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- Second line headcount was 214 against an approved establishment of 247 (33 vacancies). The financial crime function has the largest gap, with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Payments and sanctions screening is performed through a third-party vendor solution whose tuning was last reviewed in 2023. This is stale relative to the regulator's expectation of periodic tuning and testing. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Supplier A (core hosting) has an exit plan. Supplier B (fund administration platform) has no documented exit plan. Supplier C (payments and screening) has no documented exit plan, and its contract lacks a right-to-audit clause. This conflicts with the regulator's expectation of exit plans for all critical suppliers and of contractual audit rights. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Technology control gaps include incomplete privileged access review coverage in the Asian entities, vulnerability remediation SLAs missed in each of the last four quarters, and logging and monitoring that does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The Group has no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling, contrary to the regulator's expectation of an AI system inventory. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The Group's technology estate comprises 312 applications, 47 of which are business critical. It is materially fragmented following three acquisitions, with duplicate core capability in client servicing and payments. Core banking runs on a legacy on-premise platform scheduled for migration in 2027, and two CRM platforms operate in parallel across the UK and Luxembourg. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted, and wave one of the data platform cloud migration is delayed to Q2 2026. Cloud security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities. IAM is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Four FY2025 audits were deferred into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. These align with gaps identified in CL-08 and with the regulator's priorities. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- The AI governance forum referenced in the Group strategy has not yet been constituted. It is one of the forums intended to sit beneath the four executive-level forums: the Group Risk Management Committee, the Financial Crime Committee, the Technology and Operational Resilience Committee, and the Conduct and Customer Outcomes Forum. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- The Group depends on three critical technology suppliers. Exit plans exist for only one of the three, and contractual provisions on audit rights and incident notification are inconsistent across the supplier estate. The Board requested a remediation plan by the end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Important business services have been identified, but impact tolerances are still being set. Third-party dependency mapping for the four most critical services remained incomplete, and the Board requested a further update. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- The core platform modernisation programme is about four months behind baseline. Wave one of the data platform migration has slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend has increased by GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- Programme assurance over the modernisation programme found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of reported status. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Reported theme: security baselines are applied inconsistently across regional entities, with the Asian entities materially behind. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Reported theme: weak discipline in vulnerability remediation, with service levels repeatedly missed. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Reported theme: third-party exit planning is absent for two of the three critical suppliers, which represents an operational resilience and outsourcing exposure. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Reported theme: identity and access management is fragmented, with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]

### Peer enforcement and thematic read-across

- The regulator's thematic review examined financial crime controls at fourteen fund administration, depositary and institutional services firms. It focused on the onboarding of complex corporate and fund structures, which is relevant to Meridian's fund administration activity. [EX-02, Scope]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at 9 of the 14 firms, which relied on client attestation without independent verification beyond the second ownership layer. EDD triggers were applied inconsistently across multi-entity and multi-jurisdiction relationships. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At 8 of the 14 firms, sanctions screening had not been tuned or independently tested in the prior 24 months, and fuzzy-matching thresholds lacked documented rationale. Meridian's screening tuning was last reviewed in 2023 (CL-08) and the tuning audit has been carried forward (CL-09), indicating a similar exposure. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rules were largely inherited from legacy systems and not recalibrated to client mix. At 11 of the 14 firms, financial crime resourcing lagged onboarding growth, with growth targets accepted without control capacity plans. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report the outcome to their boards. Firms whose growth depends on onboarding more complex structures must show that control capability has been scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider was subject to enforcement action for financial crime control failings over a four-year period. A financial penalty was imposed, and the firm accepted a requirement to appoint an independent skilled person. [EX-03, Summary]
- The peer expanded its fund services business without commensurate investment in financial crime resourcing. Customer due diligence files for complex structures were incomplete in a material proportion of the sample tested. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The peer's sanctions screening had not been subject to an independent tuning review for three years. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- Supervisors have written to firms in the sector asking them to consider the read-across from this action, specifically on assurance over remediation closure and on aligning growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Firms that pursue growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to experience supervisory intervention. The sequencing of strategy and control investment is a differentiator of supervisory outcome. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- The sector faces margin pressure, high compliance costs and technology debt arising from consolidation, with mid-size firms disproportionately affected. [EX-04, Macro Context]
- Key themes are: financial crime control effectiveness (especially growth targeting complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions); operational resilience and concentration on a few critical technology and service providers; cyber risk (credential and third-party compromise); conduct and legacy product remediation, with redress provisions often revised upwards; technology transformation slippage; AI governance lagging deployment; and scarcity of second-line risk and compliance talent, with financial crime vacancy rates at historic highs. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]

### Operational resilience, third-party, consumer outcomes, data/AI exposures and governance

- The Board identifies the following as principal risks for the coming year: financial crime (AML/sanctions, especially in Institutional Services, where onboarding volumes have grown sharply); operational resilience, arising from concentration on a few critical third-party technology providers; conduct and customer outcomes in Retail Banking, arising from legacy product governance weaknesses; data protection and privacy, arising from cloud migration across jurisdictions; technology change risk from core platform modernisation; and emerging AI risk, where the governance framework remains under development. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- FY2024 principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology/cyber and regulatory change risk. Conduct risk was only an emerging risk, and AI was not a discrete risk category. This indicates that conduct and AI exposures were elevated in FY2025. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- In Q4 2025, Meridian's client servicing platforms were disrupted for approximately 31 hours, affecting a segment of retail and institutional clients. The disruption was attributed to a failure at a third-party hosting provider. Meridian stated that no client data was compromised and that the incident was reported to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Meridian has recognised a GBP 31m provision for the legacy product governance review in Retail Banking. The review of affected customers is ongoing and the final redress population has not yet been determined, so the provision may be understated or subject to revision. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, cost-to-income rose to 71.2% from 67.8%, and CET1 fell to 14.1% from 14.9%. The decline is attributed to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- Meridian operates a three lines of defence model, with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. In FY2024 the Board had eleven directors (six of them independent NEDs), the Audit Committee met seven times and the Risk Committee met six times. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- The Group CRO post has been vacant since November 2025, with Deputy CRO Hannah Okafor providing interim cover. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The Group Head of Compliance, Martin Ashby, is accountable for the financial crime remediation portfolio. The new CEO, Alanna Reyes (appointed February 2026), has a mandate that includes completing the regulatory remediation agenda. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The Board discussed the forthcoming departure of the Group CRO, agreed to initiate an external search and approved interim arrangements. [CL-05, Item 9 — Chief Risk Officer Succession]
- There is no permanent Group Chief Risk Officer in post; the Deputy CRO attends Risk Committee meetings in the CRO's absence. This indicates a leadership gap and a succession exposure. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- The Risk Committee has five members (four of them independent NEDs) and meets at least six times a year. It oversees risk appetite, the risk framework across the three lines of defence, and the principal risks: financial crime, operational resilience, technology/cyber, conduct and regulatory compliance. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- The Committee is mandated to review the adequacy of resourcing in the risk and compliance functions and to oversee emerging risks, including AI adoption. [CL-07, Responsibilities]
- Group Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and has historically co-sourced specialist technology, cyber and financial crime audit work. The Audit Committee asked management to review the sustainability of the co-source arrangement given the expanded FY2026 plan. [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- The FY2026 plan expands coverage of financial crime, operational resilience, third-party management, cloud security and AI governance. The Chief Internal Auditor stated that full delivery requires additional specialist resource beyond the current establishment, and that a co-source arrangement is being considered. [CL-06, Item 6 — Internal Audit Plan FY2026]
- The FY2026 plan comprises 38 audits and is built on a risk-based approach that references principal risks, supervisory priorities, prior findings and the change portfolio. [CL-09, Planning Basis]
- Planned coverage includes: financial crime (CDD, EDD, sanctions screening effectiveness and tuning, transaction monitoring, and closure validation of the remediation programme); operational resilience (including third-party dependency mapping); third-party risk (including exit planning and concentration risk); technology/cyber; conduct; and data/privacy (cross-border transfers, retention and regulatory reporting data quality). [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The AI governance readiness review is deferred pending establishment of the framework, so AI governance will not be independently assured in FY2026. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The plan requires about 6,200 audit days against internal capacity of 4,900. The shortfall of approximately 1,300 days is to be met through co-sourcing, principally in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- Meridian announced a multi-year core platform modernisation (indicatively GBP 180m over four years) and an expansion of Institutional Services (fund administration and depositary in Luxembourg and Singapore). Both increase technology change risk and financial crime/onboarding exposure. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- Meridian's cost-to-income ratio deteriorated year on year and is above the peer median. The 2028 efficiency target depends on delivering a technology programme that is already reporting slippage. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Financial crime regulatory overhang is likely to constrain inorganic options and the approval of entity expansion. The Institutional Services growth ambition concentrates the Group in the activity attracting the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- The analyst regards execution of the control agenda as the principal determinant of the medium-term equity story, and expects increased spend on external assurance and advisory during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Mid-size firms face a structural squeeze between rising compliance cost and subscale distribution, with continued consolidation expected over 36 months. [EX-08, Thesis]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of the financial crime remediation programme: which findings remain open and at what priority

- **Source A:** The Q3 2025 board minutes say the programme is 'substantially complete'. All twelve high-priority findings are closed with closure evidence accepted by second line. Only three medium-priority findings remain open, due to close this quarter, and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** A press report citing sources familiar with the supervisory dialogue says supervisors do not regard the programme as complete. It says four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. A person close to the supervisor also said some findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The two accounts differ on how many findings are open (three versus four) and on their priority (medium versus high). Neither can be verified. The board minutes are a first-party record but rely on management assertion. The press report relies on anonymous sources and Meridian declined to comment. Other evidence leaves the question open. The FY2026 audit plan includes closure validation, and no independent validation was commissioned (the client deferred it). The peer enforcement case and the thematic review show that closure by management assertion is unreliable. The annual report's more cautious wording, that some workstreams extend into next year, fits the press account better than the minutes. Meridian's supervisory correspondence sits in a confidential annex and is unavailable. These points are suggestive but do not let us pick a side. Treat the status as contested pending independent closure validation or sight of the supervisor correspondence.
