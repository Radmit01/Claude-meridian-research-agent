# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Principal Risks Identified by the Board and Management

- Board identifies principal risks for the coming year: financial crime risk (AML and sanctions, especially Institutional Services where onboarding volumes grew sharply); operational resilience risk from concentration on few critical third-party tech providers; conduct and customer outcomes risk in Retail Banking from legacy product governance weaknesses; data protection/privacy risk from cloud migration across jurisdictions; technology change risk; and emerging AI governance risk (framework still under development). [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- FY2024 principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology and cyber, and regulatory change risk. Conduct risk was only an emerging risk and AI was not a discrete risk category, so conduct and AI exposures have escalated since FY2024. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- The Committee monitors principal risks including financial crime, operational resilience, technology and cyber, conduct, and regulatory compliance risk, and oversees emerging AI risk and the adequacy of risk and compliance resourcing. [CL-07, Responsibilities]
- Top sector risks include financial crime control effectiveness (especially where growth targets complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions), operational resilience/concentration on critical providers, cyber risk (credential and third-party compromise), conduct and legacy product remediation (redress provisions often revised upward), technology transformation slippage, AI governance, and scarcity of second-line risk/compliance talent. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Mid-size firms are disproportionately affected by margin pressure, high compliance cost and technology debt, carrying comparable regulatory obligations to larger institutions without the scale. [EX-04, Macro Context]

### Regulatory Perimeter, Supervisory Engagement and Financial Impact

- Group is authorised and regulated by the FCA and PRA, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. In FY2025 it engaged with supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness, and consumer outcomes. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Regulated entities in Dublin, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong, implying multiple regulatory regimes. [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- Management states the pace of regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk, AI governance) is a material demand on second and third line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, attributed principally to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment, and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. Cost-to-income ratio rose to 71.2% (from 67.8%) and CET1 fell to 14.1% (from 14.9%). [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- Management guided to elevated regulatory and remediation costs continuing through FY2026, moderating thereafter. [CL-10, Guidance]
- Meridian's cost-to-income ratio deteriorated and is above peer median; the 2028 efficiency target depends on a technology programme already reporting slippage. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Analysts questioned the sustainability of the cost-reduction target given programme delays. The stated target is a 62% cost-to-income ratio by 2028. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Analyst view: regulatory overhang on financial crime is likely to constrain inorganic options and approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Execution of the control agenda is regarded as the principal determinant of the equity story; firms typically increase external assurance and advisory spend during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]

### Financial Crime Controls and Remediation (Highest-Severity Exposure)

- Board was told the programme is substantially complete: all twelve high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remediated and closed with closure evidence accepted by second line; three medium-priority findings remain open, due for closure within the quarter; no high-priority findings open. Board recorded satisfaction and asked for the programme to move to formal closure and be tracked via BAU. (UNRESOLVED conflict with EX-07; see Conflicts.) [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- Management says substantial progress has been made on financial crime remediation, but certain workstreams remain in delivery and extend into the next financial year (i.e. not fully complete). (UNRESOLVED tension with CL-05 on timing.) [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- Per persons familiar with supervisory dialogue, Meridian's financial crime remediation is not regarded as complete by supervisors; four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. This is press/unnamed-source reporting. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Meridian's Q3 2025 board reporting described the programme as substantially complete with all high-priority findings closed; a source close to the supervisor said some findings reported closed were reopened after independent validation because closure evidence was insufficient. Group declined to comment on supervisory correspondence and described remediation as 'well advanced and moving to completion' in its investor presentation. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Independent validation of financial crime remediation closure was under consideration but deferred pending completion of the programme. The remediation therefore has no independent closure validation yet. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- The FY2026 audit plan includes sanctions screening effectiveness and tuning, CDD/EDD, transaction monitoring, and closure validation of the remediation programme. This indicates the remediation is not yet validated as closed. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Management describes the regulatory remediation agenda to investors as "well advanced and moving to completion". This may conflict with internal evidence of unvalidated or open remediation. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- Analysts questioned management on the regulatory position on financial crime controls. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Board approved an increase in the onboarding volume appetite threshold in Institutional Services, on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and assessed as effective. This creates exposure if the remediation assurance is wrong. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Sanctions screening runs through a third-party vendor solution, and its tuning was last reviewed in 2023. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Four FY2025 audits were deferred into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at client request and carried forward. Access to the second-line financial crime team was limited because that scope was retained in-house. Financial crime controls therefore received limited independent assurance. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Access to the second-line financial crime team was limited during the year, as that scope was retained in-house. [PV-05, Relationship Observations]
- Second line headcount 214 vs approved establishment of 247; financial crime function has the largest gap with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]

### External Supervisory Expectations and Enforcement Precedent

- Firms must show CDD, EDD for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring are effective in practice. Supervisors stress periodic tuning and testing and the adequacy of financial crime resourcing. Control capability must scale ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Thematic review of 14 fund/institutional services firms found beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures inadequate at 9 of 14; firms relied on client attestation without independent verification beyond the second layer of ownership. (Sector review, not Meridian-specific.) [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- EDD triggers were inconsistently applied, particularly where a client relationship spanned multiple entities or jurisdictions within the same group. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Sanctions screening configurations had not been tuned or independently tested within 24 months at 8 firms; fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked documented rationale. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rule sets were largely inherited from legacy implementations and not recalibrated for changes in client mix. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Financial crime resourcing had not kept pace with onboarding growth at 11 firms; several accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Closure of remediation findings was in several cases evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report the outcome to their boards; where growth depends on more onboarding of complex structures, firms should evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance. (Thematic sector review, not Meridian-specific.) [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider was subject to enforcement for financial crime control failings over four years, receiving a financial penalty and a requirement to appoint an independent skilled person. [EX-03, Summary]
- Peer expanded fund services without commensurate financial crime resourcing; CDD files for complex structures were incomplete in a material proportion of the sample; sanctions screening had no independent tuning review for three years. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- Peer told its board remediation was complete when independent testing found findings closed without adequate evidence; the supervisor called inaccurate internal board reporting the most serious aggravating factor, delaying corrective action by about 18 months. Boards must satisfy themselves on closure evidence quality rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider the read-across, specifically on remediation closure assurance and alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Analysts noted that the discrepancy, if confirmed, would echo the peer enforcement action in which inaccurate board reporting was an aggravating factor. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Firms pursuing growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to experience supervisory intervention; sequencing is a differentiator of supervisory outcome. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- The institutional services growth ambition concentrates Meridian in the activity attracting the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Group announced a multi-year core platform modernisation (indicative GBP 180m over four years) and expansion of Institutional Services in Luxembourg and Singapore, which is the segment later flagged for financial crime risk. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- Supervisors are moving toward demonstrable effectiveness testing over framework documentation. They expect independent validation of control performance and of remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]

### Operational Resilience and Third-Party/Outsourcing Exposure

- Firms must have identified important business services, set impact tolerances, mapped supporting resources including third parties, and completed scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most common gap and is considered a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Important business services identified, but impact tolerances still being set, and third-party dependency mapping for the four most critical services is incomplete; Board requested further update. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Dependency mapping support was not taken up. The client indicated it would deliver this internally in the first instance. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- A ~31-hour Q4 2025 disruption to client servicing platforms affecting retail and institutional clients was attributed to a third-party hosting provider failure; Meridian said no client data was compromised and that the incident was reported to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Firms must keep a complete register of material outsourcing, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Group depends on three critical technology suppliers; exit plans exist for only one; audit rights and incident notification provisions are inconsistent across suppliers. Board requested a remediation plan by end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Supplier B (fund administration) and Supplier C (payments and screening) have no documented exit plan, and Supplier C's contract has no right-to-audit clause. This conflicts with supervisory expectations for exit plans and audit rights. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Exit planning is absent for two of three critical suppliers. Third-party onboarding and monitoring was also audited. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Exit planning for critical suppliers was scoped but not commissioned, consistent with the exit planning gap in PV-05. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT providers: minimum contractual provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]

### Technology, Cyber, Cloud and Data Protection Exposure

- Privileged access review coverage is incomplete in the Asian entities. Vulnerability remediation SLAs were missed in each of the last four quarters. Logging and monitoring do not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Vulnerability remediation discipline is weak, with service levels missed repeatedly. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- IAM is fragmented, with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted. Security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities, and IAM is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Security baselines are applied inconsistently across regional entities, with the Asian entities materially behind. Cloud security configuration (UK and Luxembourg) was among the audits delivered. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- The estate has 312 applications, 47 of them business critical. It is fragmented by three acquisitions, with duplicate client servicing and payments capability. The core banking migration is scheduled for 2027 and data platform cloud wave one is delayed to Q2 2026. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- Core platform modernisation is about four months behind baseline; data migration wave one slipped to Q2 2026; forecast spend up GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- Programme assurance found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of status. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Scrutiny is increasing on cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and use of personal data in training and operating AI systems. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- A data protection and cross-border transfers audit was delivered in FY2024. [PV-05, Audits Delivered]
- The FY2026 audit plan covers operational resilience (including third-party dependency mapping), third-party risk, cyber, conduct (including redress), and data and privacy (cross-border transfers, retention, regulatory reporting data quality). [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]

### Conduct and Consumer Outcomes Exposure

- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including legacy products no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend into legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectation that firms identify affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]
- GBP 31m provision recognised for legacy product governance review in Retail Banking; affected customer review ongoing and final redress population not yet determined, so the provision may be understated. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Analysts questioned the size and certainty of the legacy redress provision. Management said the redress population remains under review and a further provision cannot be ruled out. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]

### AI Governance Exposure

- Firms deploying AI must keep an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors say firms should not scale deployment ahead of establishing governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Emerging requirements converge on AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency to affected individuals, bias and drift testing, and senior-management accountability. Firms using AI in onboarding, credit or advice face the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- The AI governance forum referenced in the Group strategy has not yet been constituted, and the Committee's FY2025 effectiveness review found no defined framework for governing artificial intelligence. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- There is no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling across the Group, while the regulator expects an AI system inventory. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The AI governance readiness review is deferred pending establishment of the framework. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- AI governance framework design was discussed only at an exploratory level with the CTO, so no framework is evidenced as commissioned. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]

### Governance, Leadership and Assurance Capacity

- Group Chief Risk Officer position vacant since November 2025, with Deputy CRO Hannah Okafor providing interim cover. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- There is no permanent Group Chief Risk Officer; the Deputy CRO attends Committee meetings in the absence of a permanent appointment. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- Board discussed the forthcoming departure of the Group CRO, agreed to an external search and approved interim arrangements. [CL-05, Item 9 — Chief Risk Officer Succession]
- Group Head of Compliance Martin Ashby is accountable for the financial crime remediation portfolio; new CEO Alanna Reyes (appointed Feb 2026) has a mandate including completion of the regulatory remediation agenda; COO Devan Mistry is accountable for platform modernisation. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The FY2025 effectiveness review identified three gaps: inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and no defined AI governance framework. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- FY2024: Board of eleven directors (six independent NEDs); Audit Committee met seven times and Risk Committee six; three lines of defence model with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and relies on co-sourcing for specialist technology, cyber and financial crime audit work; Audit Committee has asked management to review sustainability of the co-source arrangement given the expanded FY2026 plan. [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- FY2026 audit plan expands coverage to financial crime, operational resilience, third-party management, cloud security and AI governance; full delivery requires additional specialist resource beyond establishment and a co-source arrangement is under consideration. [CL-06, Item 6 — Internal Audit Plan FY2026]
- The plan needs about 6,200 audit days against 4,900 internal capacity, a shortfall of about 1,300 days to be met through co-source arrangements in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- The client had not confirmed whether the FY2026 co-source would be extended, expanded to include financial crime disciplines, or re-tendered. [PV-09, Open Items at Time of Writing]

### Recommended Engagement Focus Areas

- Prioritise independent validation of financial crime remediation closure. The board record (twelve high-priority findings closed) and press reporting (four high-priority findings open, some reopened) cannot be reconciled on the evidence. Neither side has independent validation, so the status should be treated as unresolved and tested directly with supervisory correspondence and closure evidence. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Test the quality of closure evidence behind the board's 'substantially complete' conclusion. The peer enforcement precedent shows inaccurate board reporting was the most serious aggravating factor and delayed corrective action by about 18 months. The same pattern is alleged for Meridian. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Examine the board's approved increase in Institutional Services onboarding appetite, which was granted on the basis that financial crime controls were remediated and effective. If the remediation assurance is wrong, Meridian is growing into the activity under the greatest scrutiny ahead of control capacity. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Clarify the scope and dating of the CL-05 and CL-01 remediation statements. One says the remainder will close within the quarter and the other says workstreams extend into the next financial year. Establish whether they cover the same population of findings. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- Focus on the limited independent assurance over financial crime. Closure validation was deferred pending programme completion, so no independent validation has been done. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Prioritise sanctions screening tuning. It was last reviewed in 2023, the audit has been deferred, and peer and thematic evidence treats stale tuning as a recurring supervisory finding. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Assess second-line financial crime capacity. It has the largest vacancy gap at 19 roles, and the CRO post is vacant. Thematic evidence says resourcing that lags onboarding growth is a common failing. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Concentrate on third-party concentration and exit planning. Exit plans exist for only one of three critical suppliers, Supplier C has no right-to-audit clause, and the client has not commissioned the exit planning work. This falls short of the stated supervisory expectations. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Follow up on the incomplete dependency mapping for the four most critical services and the unset impact tolerances. The Q4 2025 hosting provider outage shows the exposure is live, and supervisors treat incomplete mapping as a material shortcoming. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Treat the absence of an AI governance framework and a model inventory as a gap against supervisory expectations. The governance forum has not been constituted, the readiness review is deferred, and no framework work has been commissioned. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Examine the GBP 31m legacy redress provision, which may be understated. The affected population is not yet determined, and analysts say a further provision cannot be ruled out. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Review cyber and cloud control weaknesses, particularly in the Asian entities. These include incomplete privileged access reviews, repeated missed vulnerability SLAs and inconsistent security baselines. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Seek access to the confidential supervisor correspondence annex. It may settle the remediation conflict and show the true regulatory position, but its content is not available in the corpus. [CL-06, Item 7 — Regulatory Correspondence]
- Address the internal audit shortfall of about 1,300 days and the unconfirmed co-source scope. Without it, the deferred audits on sanctions, third-party exit and model risk may slip further. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- Assess the effect of modernisation slippage on the 2028 cost-to-income target and on remediation dependencies. The programme is about four months behind and forecast spend is up GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of Meridian's financial crime remediation: whether any high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open

- **Source A:** The board was told all twelve high-priority findings were remediated and closed, with closure evidence accepted by second line. Only three medium-priority findings remain open and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Press reporting, citing persons familiar with supervisory dialogue, says supervisors do not regard remediation as complete and four high-priority findings remain open. A source close to the supervisor also says some findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** Neither source can be preferred on the evidence. CL-05 is Meridian's own board record but rests on management assertion. EX-07 is press reporting from unnamed sources, and the Group declined to comment on it. PV-09 says no independent closure validation has taken place yet. CL-09 plans closure validation, which also implies closure has not been independently validated. No supervisor document or independent validation result is available to settle it. The discrepancy should be reported as unresolved.

**Subject:** Timing and completeness of financial crime remediation (substantially complete with the remainder closing within the quarter, versus workstreams extending into the next financial year)

- **Source A:** The programme is substantially complete. Only three medium-priority findings remain, due for closure within the quarter, and the board asked for the programme to move to formal closure and BAU tracking. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Substantial progress has been made, but certain financial crime workstreams remain in delivery and extend into the next financial year. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The two statements may differ only in scope: CL-05 refers to supervisory findings and CL-01 to remediation workstreams. The documents are also dated differently, and the findings do not give the CL-05 meeting date. The evidence does not show whether they describe the same population at different times or are genuinely incompatible, so I cannot pick a side.
