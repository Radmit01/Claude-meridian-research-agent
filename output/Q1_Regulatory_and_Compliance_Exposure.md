# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Financial Crime (AML, Sanctions, CDD) and Remediation Status

- The Board lists financial crime risk (AML and sanctions compliance) as a principal risk, particularly in Institutional Services, where onboarding volumes have grown sharply. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Management states that substantial progress has been made across the financial crime remediation portfolio, but that certain workstreams remain in delivery and extend into the next financial year. This is softer than the board minutes' "substantially complete" and is a possible conflict signal. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- The Board was told the programme is substantially complete: all 12 high-priority supervisory review findings have been remediated and closed, and accepted by the second line. Three medium-priority findings remain open and are due for closure within the quarter. The Group Head of Compliance confirmed that no high-priority findings remain open. This conflicts with the external press report in EX-07, which states that four findings remain open. The conflict is unresolved. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- According to persons familiar with the supervisory dialogue, supervisors do not regard the financial crime remediation programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- This contradicts Q3 2025 board reporting, which described the programme as substantially complete with all high-priority findings closed. A source close to the supervisor said that some findings previously reported as closed were reopened after independent validation because the closure evidence was insufficient. The report is based on unnamed sources, and the Group declined to comment. The conflict is not resolved: the evidence cannot establish which account is correct. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The Chair asked that the programme be prepared for formal closure and that the remaining medium-priority items move to business-as-usual tracking. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- The Board approved an increase in the onboarding volume appetite threshold in Institutional Services on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and assessed as effective. If the remediation claim is wrong, this decision rests on a faulty premise and raises AML exposure. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- The client considered independent validation of financial crime remediation closure but deferred it pending completion of the programme, which indicates that the remediation was not yet complete at the time of writing. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- The FY2026 internal audit plan includes closure validation of the financial crime remediation programme, indicating that independent validation is planned. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Sanctions screening is performed via a third-party vendor solution, and tuning was last reviewed in 2023. This is stale relative to supervisory expectations of periodic tuning and testing. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at the client's request and carried forward. This relates to an area flagged as an open supervisory finding elsewhere. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Four FY2025 audits were deferred into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. These are the same areas where control gaps are noted elsewhere. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- Access to the second line financial crime team was limited because that scope was retained in-house; as a result, Protiviti's audit coverage of financial crime was limited. [PV-05, Relationship Observations]
- The Group Head of Compliance, Martin Ashby, is accountable for the financial crime remediation portfolio. The new CEO's stated mandate includes completion of the regulatory remediation agenda. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- Second line headcount was 214 at year end against an approved establishment of 247, a gap of 33. The financial crime function carries the largest vacancy gap, with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]

### Supervisory Expectations and Sector Benchmarks (Financial Crime, Resilience, Third Party, Consumer, Data, AI)

- Supervisors expect CDD, EDD for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice, with emphasis on periodic tuning and testing and on adequate financial crime resourcing. Control capability must be scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Firms must have identified important business services, set impact tolerances, mapped supporting resources (including third parties), and completed scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is considered a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to hold a complete register of material outsourcing, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Firms deploying AI must maintain an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors say firms should not scale deployment ahead of governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at 9 of 14 firms reviewed, with reliance on client attestation and no independent verification beyond the second layer. EDD triggers were inconsistently applied where relationships span multiple entities or jurisdictions. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At 8 firms, sanctions screening had not been tuned or independently tested in the prior 24 months, and fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked a documented rationale. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rules were largely inherited and had not been recalibrated to client mix. Financial crime resourcing lagged onboarding growth at 11 firms, with growth targets accepted without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Closure of remediation findings was often evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report to their boards. Where growth depends on onboarding more complex structures, firms must evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- Emerging AI requirements include an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight, transparency, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. AI used in onboarding, credit or advice attracts the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT providers. They cover contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- Scrutiny is increasing of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in AI training and operation. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- Supervisors are moving towards demonstrable effectiveness testing, and expect independent validation of both control performance and remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- Outcomes-based obligations are extending to legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectations around identifying affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]
- Protiviti's methodology treats closure validation as a distinct assurance activity. A finding is regarded as closed only where the underlying control is demonstrated to operate effectively, tested independently against the original finding rather than by management assertion. This serves as a benchmark for assessing management claims that remediation is complete. [PV-04, Closure Validation Principle]
- Protiviti validates closure of actions through independent evidence testing rather than management assertion. [PV-04, Phase 5 — Reporting and Follow-Up]
- The sector faces margin pressure, an elevated cost of regulatory compliance and technology debt. Mid-size firms are disproportionately affected, as they lack scale while carrying comparable regulatory obligations. [EX-04, Macro Context]
- Financial crime control effectiveness is a top sector risk theme, particularly where growth strategies target complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Operational resilience risk, and concentration on a small number of critical technology and service providers, is a top sector risk theme. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Cyber risk is a top theme, with credential compromise and third-party compromise the dominant incident vectors. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Conduct and legacy product remediation is a top theme, with redress provisions frequently revised upwards after initial recognition. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- AI deployment is materially outpacing control frameworks, and most multi-year core migrations report schedule slippage. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Second line risk and compliance roles are scarce, and vacancy rates in financial crime functions are at historic highs. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Firms that pursue growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to experience supervisory intervention. The sequencing of strategy and control investment differentiates supervisory outcomes. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]

### Enforcement Precedent and Board Reporting Accuracy

- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider received a financial penalty and was required to appoint an independent skilled person over financial crime failings spanning four years. [EX-03, Summary]
- The peer expanded fund services without matching financial crime resourcing. Its CDD files for complex structures were incomplete in a material share of the sample, and its sanctions screening had no independent tuning review for three years. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The peer told its board that a remediation programme was complete, but independent testing found that findings had been closed without adequate evidence. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor treated inaccurate internal reporting to the board as the most serious aggravating factor, noting that it delayed corrective action by about 18 months. Boards must satisfy themselves on closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider the read-across, specifically remediation closure assurance and the alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Analysts note that the discrepancy, if confirmed, would echo a peer enforcement action in which inaccurate internal board reporting was an aggravating factor. The Group's investor presentation describes its remediation agenda as 'well advanced and moving to completion'. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Regulatory overhang, particularly in financial crime, is likely to constrain inorganic options and the approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- The institutional services growth ambition concentrates the Group in the activity attracting the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Execution of the control agenda is regarded as the principal determinant of the equity story. Firms in this position typically increase spend on external assurance and advisory support during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Mid-size firms face a squeeze between rising compliance costs and subscale distribution, with continued consolidation expected over 36 months. [EX-08, Thesis]

### Operational Resilience, Third-Party Concentration and Outsourcing

- Operational resilience risk arising from concentration on a small number of critical third-party technology providers is a principal risk. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Important business services have been identified, but impact tolerances are still being set. Third-party dependency mapping for the four most critical services is incomplete. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- The Group depends on three critical technology suppliers. Exit plans exist for only one of the three, and audit rights and incident notification provisions are inconsistent across the supplier estate. The Board requested a remediation plan by the end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Supplier B (fund administration platform) has no documented exit plan. Supplier C (payments and screening) has no documented exit plan, and its contract lacks a right-to-audit clause. Only Supplier A (core hosting) has an exit plan. This conflicts with regulatory expectations of documented exit plans for all critical suppliers and contractual audit rights. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Third-party exit planning was absent for two of the three critical suppliers. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Support for operational resilience dependency mapping was to be delivered internally by the client in the first instance. Third-party exit planning for critical suppliers was scoped but not commissioned. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- In Q4 2025, Meridian suffered a disruption of approximately 31 hours to its client servicing platforms, attributed to a third-party hosting provider failure. The Group states that no client data was compromised and that supervisors were notified. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The FY2025 effectiveness review found inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities and limited aggregation of third-party risk data. [CL-07, Known Gaps]

### Technology, Cyber, Cloud and Data Protection

- Data protection and privacy risk arises from the migration of customer data to cloud platforms across multiple jurisdictions. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Technology change risk is associated with the multi-year core platform modernisation programme. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Core platform modernisation is about four months behind baseline, wave one of the data platform migration has slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend has risen by GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- Privileged access review coverage is incomplete in the Asian entities, vulnerability remediation SLAs were missed in each of the last four quarters, and logging and monitoring does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted. Cloud security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities. Identity and access management (IAM) is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. Wave one of the data platform cloud migration has been delayed to Q2 2026. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- The estate comprises 312 applications, 47 of which are business critical, and is fragmented by three acquisitions, with duplicate core capability in client servicing and payments. Core banking is a legacy on-premise platform scheduled for migration in 2027, and fund administration is hosted by a third party in Luxembourg. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- Security baselines were applied inconsistently across regional entities, with the Asian entities materially behind. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Vulnerability remediation discipline was weak, with service levels missed repeatedly. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Identity and access management (IAM) was fragmented, with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Programme assurance found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of status. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- The 2028 efficiency target depends on a technology programme that is already reporting slippage. The cost-to-income ratio has deteriorated and is above the peer median. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- The Group COO, Devan Mistry, is accountable for the core platform modernisation programme. A new Group CEO, Alanna Reyes, was appointed in February 2026. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- Fourteen of the fifteen planned audits were delivered in year one, including those covering data protection and cross-border transfers and third-party onboarding and monitoring. [PV-05, Audits Delivered]

### Conduct, Consumer Outcomes and Redress

- Conduct and customer outcomes risk in Retail Banking, following the identification of legacy product governance weaknesses, is a principal risk. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, attributed principally to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. The cost-to-income ratio rose to 71.2%, and CET1 fell to 14.1% from 14.9%. [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- A GBP 31m provision has been recognised for the legacy product governance review in Retail Banking. The review of affected customers is ongoing and the final redress population has not been determined, so the provision may prove insufficient. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- In FY2024, the Group's principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology and cyber, and regulatory change risk. Conduct risk was classed only as an emerging risk, and AI was not a discrete risk category. Exposures relating to conduct and AI have since been elevated. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]

### AI Governance

- AI adoption in customer-facing and decisioning processes is an emerging risk, and the Group's governance framework for it remains under development. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- The AI governance forum referenced in Group strategy has not been constituted. The FY2025 Risk Committee effectiveness review identified the absence of a defined AI governance framework as a gap. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- The Group has no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling. This conflicts with the supervisory expectation that an AI system inventory be maintained. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The AI governance readiness review has been deferred pending establishment of the framework, so there is no assurance over AI governance in FY2026. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- AI governance framework design was discussed only at an exploratory level with the Chief Technology Officer, so no framework appears to be in place or commissioned. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]

### Governance, Regulatory Perimeter, Leadership and Assurance Capacity

- The Group is authorised and regulated by the FCA and PRA, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. In FY2025 it engaged with supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness and consumer outcomes. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Management states that the pace of regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk and AI governance) places a material demand on second and third line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Meridian operates regulated regional entities in Dublin (Meridian Financial Services (Ireland) DAC), Luxembourg (Meridian Fund Services S.A.), Singapore (Meridian Asia Pte Ltd) and Hong Kong (Meridian Capital Markets (HK) Limited), which implies multiple regulatory regimes. [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- The Board had 11 directors, 6 of whom were independent NEDs. The Audit Committee met 7 times and the Risk Committee 6 times. The Group operates a three lines of defence model, with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- The Group announced a GBP 180m, four-year core platform modernisation and an expansion of Institutional Services (fund administration and depositary) in Luxembourg and Singapore. These are drivers of technology change and financial crime exposure. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- The Group CRO is departing; an external search will be initiated and interim arrangements have been approved. This represents a leadership gap in the risk function. [CL-05, Item 9 — Chief Risk Officer Succession]
- The Group Chief Risk Officer position has been vacant since November 2025. Interim cover is provided by the Deputy CRO, Hannah Okafor. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- There is currently no permanent CRO appointment, and the Deputy CRO attends Risk Committee meetings in the CRO's absence. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- The Risk Committee monitors principal risks, including financial crime, operational resilience, technology and cyber, conduct and regulatory compliance risk, and oversees the adequacy of resourcing in risk and compliance functions. [CL-07, Responsibilities]
- The FY2026 internal audit plan expands coverage of financial crime, operational resilience, third-party management, cloud security and AI governance. Full delivery requires additional specialist resource beyond the current establishment, and a co-source arrangement is being considered. [CL-06, Item 6 — Internal Audit Plan FY2026]
- Group Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and has historically co-sourced specialist technology, cyber and financial crime audit work. The Audit Committee has asked management to review the sustainability of the co-source arrangement in light of the expanded FY2026 plan. [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- The FY2026 plan of 38 audits covers financial crime (CDD, EDD, sanctions screening tuning, transaction monitoring and closure validation of remediation), operational resilience, third-party risk, technology and cyber, conduct, and data/privacy. This indicates that these are recognised principal exposure areas. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The plan requires about 6,200 audit days against internal capacity of 4,900. The shortfall of about 1,300 days is to be met through co-source arrangements, principally in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- The client had not confirmed whether the FY2026 co-source arrangement would be extended, expanded to include financial crime disciplines, or re-tendered. This creates uncertainty over assurance coverage of financial crime. [PV-09, Open Items at Time of Writing]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of the financial crime remediation programme: how many findings remain open, and of what priority

- **Source A:** The board was told the programme is substantially complete. All 12 high-priority supervisory review findings are remediated, closed and accepted by second line. Only 3 medium-priority findings remain open. The Group Head of Compliance confirmed no high-priority findings remain open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Per unnamed persons familiar with the supervisory dialogue, supervisors do not regard the programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. Some findings previously reported as closed were reopened after independent validation found the closure evidence insufficient. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The evidence does not let me pick a side. CL-05 is a primary internal record, but it is management's own assertion to the board. EX-07 is a later press report resting on unnamed sources, and the Group declined to comment. Recency would favour EX-07, but a later date does not make unverified sourcing more reliable. Other documents lean toward EX-07 without settling it. CL-01 uses softer wording (some workstreams extend into next year). PV-09 says independent closure validation was deferred pending programme completion. CL-09 plans closure validation. EX-02 and EX-03 show that management-asserted closure is often unreliable. However, PV-09 says independent validation had not yet been done, which sits awkwardly with EX-07's claim that independent validation reopened findings. No source provides the supervisory finding register or independent test results, so the discrepancy should be reported as unresolved and flagged for verification.
