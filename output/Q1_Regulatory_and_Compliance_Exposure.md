# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Principal risks and regulatory perimeter

- The Board has identified the following principal risks for the coming year: financial crime (AML/sanctions), particularly in Institutional Services, where onboarding volumes have grown sharply; operational resilience, given concentration on a small number of critical third-party technology providers; conduct and customer outcomes in Retail Banking, owing to weaknesses in legacy product governance; data protection and privacy, arising from cloud migration across jurisdictions; technology change, driven by core platform modernisation; and emerging AI risk, with the governance framework still under development. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- The Group is authorised and regulated by the FCA and PRA, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. During FY2025 it engaged with supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness and consumer outcomes. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Management states that the pace of regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk and AI governance) places a material demand on second- and third-line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- The FY2024 principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology and cyber, and regulatory change risk. Conduct risk was only an emerging risk, and AI was not a discrete risk category; conduct and AI exposures were therefore elevated in FY2025. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- The regulated entities include Meridian Financial Services (Ireland) DAC, Meridian Fund Services S.A. (Luxembourg), Meridian Asia Pte Ltd (Singapore) and Meridian Capital Markets (HK) Limited. [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- The Committee monitors the principal risks of financial crime, operational resilience, technology and cyber, conduct, and regulatory compliance. There is no permanent Group CRO; the Deputy CRO attends in their absence. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- The Committee reviews the adequacy of resourcing in the risk and compliance functions and oversees emerging risks, including AI adoption. [CL-07, Responsibilities]
- The top sector risks include: the effectiveness of financial crime controls (as firms grow into complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions); operational resilience and concentration on critical providers; cyber risk; conduct and legacy product remediation (with redress often revised upward); slippage in technology transformation; AI governance lagging deployment; and scarcity of second-line talent, with record vacancy rates in financial crime. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Mid-size firms face margin pressure, high compliance costs and technology debt, while carrying obligations comparable to those of larger institutions. [EX-04, Macro Context]

### Financial crime: remediation status (unresolved conflict)

- Management says substantial progress has been made across the financial crime remediation portfolio, but certain workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year. This is more cautious than the claim of 'substantially complete' in the Q3 2025 board minutes. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- The Board was told that the programme is substantially complete: all twelve high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review had been remediated and closed with second-line acceptance, and no high-priority findings remain open. Three medium-priority findings remain open and are due for closure in the current quarter. The Board asked for formal programme closure. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- Supervisors reportedly do not regard the financial crime remediation programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The press account differs materially from the Q3 2025 board reporting. A source close to the supervisor said that some findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation because the closure evidence was insufficient. The Group declined to comment on supervisory correspondence and, in its investor presentation, described remediation as 'well advanced and moving to completion'. UNRESOLVED: CL-05 is management's own account to the Board, and EX-07 relies on an unnamed source; neither is a primary independent validation, and the relative dates are not established. PV-09, PV-05, CL-08 and CL-09 are consistent with the press account but do not prove it. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The regulatory remediation agenda is described to investors as "well advanced and moving to completion". This is relevant to the conflict between sources on remediation status. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- The client considered independent validation of financial crime remediation closure but deferred it pending completion of the programme. This is relevant because supervisors expect remediation closure to be independently validated. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- The FY2026 audit plan comprises 38 audits. It covers financial crime (CDD, EDD, sanctions screening tuning, transaction monitoring and remediation closure validation), operational resilience, third-party risk, cyber, conduct, and data and privacy. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Group Head of Compliance Martin Ashby is accountable for the financial crime remediation portfolio, and COO Devan Mistry is accountable for the core platform modernisation. [CL-03, Executive Committee]

### Financial crime: control exposures and growth

- The Board approved an increased appetite threshold for onboarding volumes in Institutional Services, on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and assessed as effective. If the remediation claim is wrong, AML and financial crime exposure is heightened. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Growth in Institutional Services, focused on Luxembourg and Singapore, is expected to raise onboarding volumes of complex corporate and fund structures by about 140%. [CL-04, Four Strategic Pillars]
- The Group announced a multi-year core platform modernisation with an indicative investment of GBP 180m over four years, together with expansion of Institutional Services (fund administration and depositary services in Luxembourg and Singapore), the segment later flagged for financial crime exposure. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- The strategy depends on three enablers: completing the financial crime remediation portfolio, achieving a group-wide operational resilience capability that meets supervisory expectations, and filling second-line vacancies. Management flagged that failure to complete remediation would constrain regulatory approval for expanding the Luxembourg and Singapore entities. [CL-04, Dependencies and Enablers]
- Sanctions screening runs through a third-party vendor solution, whose tuning was last reviewed in 2023. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Four FY2025 audits were carried into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at the client's request and carried forward. Protiviti's access to the second-line financial crime team was limited because that scope was retained in-house. This is relevant because sanctions screening tuning is one of the findings reported as open. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Access to the second-line financial crime team was limited during the year, as that scope was retained in-house. [PV-05, Relationship Observations]
- Regulatory overhang, particularly in relation to financial crime, is likely to constrain inorganic growth options and the approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- The Group's institutional services growth ambition concentrates it in the activity that attracts the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Meridian's financial crime function vacancy rate is 19%, against a peer median of 11% and an upper quartile of 6%, placing it in the highest vacancy quartile. Second-line headcount is 4.5% of total headcount, against a peer median of 6.1%, indicating understaffing. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Analysts questioned management on the regulatory position in respect of financial crime controls. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]

### Supervisory expectations and enforcement precedent

- Supervisors expect customer due diligence (CDD), enhanced due diligence (EDD) for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice. They place emphasis on periodic tuning and testing, on the adequacy of financial crime resourcing, and on control capability being scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at 9 of 14 firms reviewed. Firms relied on client attestation without independent verification beyond the second ownership layer. EDD triggers were applied inconsistently across multi-entity and multi-jurisdiction groups. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At 8 firms, sanctions screening had not been tuned or independently tested within the previous 24 months, and fuzzy-matching thresholds lacked a documented rationale. Transaction monitoring rules had been inherited from legacy systems and not recalibrated to the client mix. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At 11 firms, financial crime resourcing had not kept pace with onboarding growth, and several firms accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Closure of remediation findings was often evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings, report to their boards, and evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance where growth depends on onboarding complex structures. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider faced a financial penalty and a requirement to appoint an independent skilled person, in respect of financial crime failings spanning four years. [EX-03, Summary]
- The failings were: expansion of fund services without commensurate financial crime resourcing; incomplete CDD files for complex structures; the board being told that remediation was complete when independent testing found that findings had been closed without adequate evidence; and sanctions screening that lacked an independent tuning review for three years. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor viewed inaccurate internal reporting to the board as the most serious aggravating factor, which delayed corrective action by about eighteen months. Boards must verify the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to firms in the sector asking them to consider the read-across to their own business on remediation closure assurance and on the alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Firms pursuing growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to face supervisory intervention. The sequencing of strategy and control investment differentiates supervisory outcomes. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- Analysts note that, if the discrepancy is confirmed, it would echo a recent peer enforcement action in which inaccurate internal reporting to the board was an aggravating factor. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Supervisors are moving towards demonstrable effectiveness testing in preference to framework documentation. They expect independent validation of control performance and of remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]

### Operational resilience and third-party risk

- Important business services have been identified and impact tolerances are being set. Third-party dependency mapping for the four most critical services is incomplete, and the Board has requested a further update. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- The Group depends on three critical technology suppliers. Exit plans exist for only one of them, and audit-rights and incident-notification contract terms are inconsistent across suppliers. The Board requested a remediation plan by the end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Supplier B (fund administration) and Supplier C (payments and screening) have no documented exit plan. Supplier C's contract contains no right-to-audit clause. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Third-party exit planning is absent for two of the three critical suppliers. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Third-party exit planning for critical suppliers was scoped but not commissioned. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Support for operational resilience dependency mapping was to be delivered internally in the first instance. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- In Q4 2025, a client servicing platform disruption lasting about 31 hours affected retail and institutional clients and was attributed to a third-party hosting provider failure. The Group states that no client data was compromised and that it reported the incident to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Firms must identify important business services, set impact tolerances, map supporting resources (including third parties) and complete scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most common gap and is considered a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to maintain a complete register of material outsourcing arrangements, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT providers and cover minimum contractual provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- Only 33% of Meridian's critical suppliers have documented exit plans (peer median 71%), and only 40% of important business services have completed dependency mapping (peer median 78%). These are significant gaps. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]

### Conduct, consumer outcomes and redress

- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, attributed mainly to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment, and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. CET1 fell to 14.1% from 14.9%, and cost-to-income rose to 71.2% from 67.8%. [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- A GBP 31m provision has been recognised for legacy product governance issues in Retail Banking. The review of affected customers is ongoing and the final redress population has not yet been determined, so the redress liability may increase. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Analysts questioned the size and certainty of the legacy redress provision. Management stated that the redress population remains under review and that a further provision cannot be ruled out. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products that are no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Outcomes-based obligations are extending to legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectations around identifying affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

### Technology, cyber, cloud and data protection

- The core platform programme is about four months behind baseline, data migration wave one has slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend has increased by GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- The roadmap includes core banking migration in 2027 and full cloud migration by 2028, alongside the decommissioning of legacy applications. [CL-04, Technology Roadmap Summary]
- The estate comprises 312 applications, 47 of which are business critical, and is fragmented following three acquisitions. Core banking runs on a legacy on-premise platform due to migrate in 2027, and wave one of the data platform cloud migration is delayed to Q2 2026. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- The privileged access review is incomplete in the Asian entities. Vulnerability remediation SLAs were missed in each of the last four quarters. Logging and monitoring does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Cloud security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities. Identity and access management is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Security baselines are applied inconsistently across regional entities, with the Asian entities materially behind. Vulnerability remediation is weak, with service levels repeatedly missed. Identity and access management is fragmented, with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Assurance over the modernisation programme found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of status. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- The audits delivered covered cloud security (UK and Luxembourg), IAM, vulnerability management, third-party onboarding and monitoring, data protection and cross-border transfers, and phase one of modernisation change management. Fourteen of 15 planned audits were delivered. [PV-05, Audits Delivered]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Scrutiny is increasing of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in AI training and operation. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- The 2028 efficiency target depends on a technology programme that is already reporting slippage. Cost-to-income deteriorated year on year and is above the peer median. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Cloud workload share is 34% against a peer median of 52%, placing Meridian behind peers on migration. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]

### AI governance and model risk

- The AI governance forum referenced in the Group strategy has not yet been constituted. The FY2025 effectiveness review found no defined framework for AI governance. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- The FY2025 effectiveness review identified three gaps: inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and no defined AI governance framework. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- There is no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling across the Group. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The AI governance readiness review has been deferred pending establishment of the framework. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The Group has no approved AI governance framework, AI inventory, or model risk controls covering generative AI. These are prerequisites to deployment beyond pilot scale. [CL-04, Artificial Intelligence Ambition]
- AI governance framework design was discussed with the CTO only at an exploratory level. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Firms deploying AI or generative AI are expected to maintain an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors state that firms should not scale deployment ahead of governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Emerging requirements include an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. Firms using AI in onboarding, credit or advice face the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Meridian has 0% approved AI governance framework coverage, against a peer median of 45%; no framework is in place. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]

### Governance, capacity and assurance

- The Board comprises eleven directors, six of whom are independent non-executive directors. The Audit Committee met seven times and the Risk Committee six. Meridian operates a three lines of defence model, with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- The Group CRO position has been vacant since November 2025, with interim cover provided by Deputy CRO Hannah Okafor. A new CEO, Alanna Reyes (appointed February 2026), has a mandate that includes completing the regulatory remediation agenda. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The Board discussed the forthcoming departure of the Group CRO, agreed to begin an external search and approved interim arrangements. [CL-05, Item 9 — Chief Risk Officer Succession]
- Second line headcount was 214 against an approved establishment of 247, leaving 33 vacancies. Financial crime has the largest gap, with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and co-sources specialist technology, cyber and financial crime audit work. Given the expanded FY2026 plan, the Audit Committee asked management to review the sustainability of the co-source arrangement. [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- The FY2026 audit plan expands coverage to financial crime, operational resilience, third-party management, cloud security and AI governance. Full delivery requires additional specialist resource beyond the current establishment, and a co-source arrangement is being considered. [CL-06, Item 6 — Internal Audit Plan FY2026]
- The plan requires about 6,200 audit days against 4,900 days of internal capacity. The shortfall of roughly 1,300 days is to be met through co-sourcing, mainly in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- The client had not confirmed whether the FY2026 co-source arrangement would be extended, expanded to include financial crime disciplines, or re-tendered. [PV-09, Open Items at Time of Writing]
- Audit plan days delivered were 92% of planned, compared with a peer median of 97%, indicating a slight underdelivery. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]

### Financial position, cost and market view

- Management guided that elevated regulatory and remediation costs will continue through FY2026, moderating thereafter. [CL-10, Guidance]
- Meridian's regulatory and remediation spend is 4.8% of income, against a peer median of 2.9%. Its cost-to-income ratio is 71.2% against a peer median of 66.4%, and is deteriorating. Its CET1 ratio is 14.1% against a peer median of 15.2%. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Execution of the control agenda is the principal determinant of the equity story. Firms in this position typically increase external assurance and specialist advisory spend during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]

The board minutes (all 12 high-priority findings closed) and the press report (four high-priority findings open) conflict, and the conflict is unresolved. Neither source reports independent validation, so the lack of validation is the key exposure and should be tested directly. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- Assess the accuracy and evidence quality of reporting to the Board on remediation closure. A peer enforcement action treated inaccurate board reporting as the most serious aggravating factor, and the press account suggests that Meridian's board reporting may be similarly exposed. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Review the Board's increased Institutional Services onboarding appetite. It was approved on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and were effective, a premise that is unverified and contested. If the premise is wrong, AML exposure rises alongside the roughly 140% growth in complex-structure onboarding. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Focus on sanctions screening tuning. It was last reviewed in 2023, the related audit was deferred at the client's request, and it is one of the findings reported as open. Supervisors expect periodic tuning and independent testing. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Examine financial crime second-line capacity. The vacancy rate is 19% against a peer median of 11%, with 19 unfilled roles, while growth is planned. Peers' resourcing failing to keep pace with onboarding growth was a supervisory finding. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Assess the dependency of the Luxembourg and Singapore expansion on completing remediation. Management itself flags that incomplete remediation would constrain regulatory approval, and sector analysis shows that growth ahead of control remediation raises the likelihood of supervisory intervention. [CL-04, Dependencies and Enablers]
- Prioritise third-party exit planning, audit rights and dependency mapping. Exit plans exist for only 33% of critical suppliers (peer median 71%), one supplier has no right-to-audit clause, and the Q4 2025 outage of about 31 hours was attributed to a third-party hosting provider. Privileged access review is incomplete in the Asian entities, vulnerability remediation SLAs were missed for four consecutive quarters, and baselines are applied inconsistently across jurisdictions. These gaps sit alongside supervisory scrutiny of cross-border transfers and cloud data. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Seek access to the confidential annex covering supervisor correspondence received in the quarter. Its content, and any associated regulatory exposure, are unknown, and it may bear directly on the remediation conflict. [CL-06, Item 7 — Regulatory Correspondence]
- Consider the weakened risk governance. The CRO seat has been vacant since November 2025, and the internal audit plan has a shortfall of roughly 1,300 days, to be covered by co-sourcing that the client has not confirmed. Together, these factors limit independent assurance over remediation. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- Scope support for independent validation of remediation closure, financial crime co-source and exit planning. The client deferred or retained in-house several of these, and the co-source extension is unconfirmed, so these remain open areas for the engagement team. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of the financial crime remediation programme: whether high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review are closed

- **Source A:** The Board was told the programme is substantially complete. All twelve high-priority findings were remediated and closed with second line acceptance. Only three medium-priority findings remain open, and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Supervisors reportedly do not regard the programme as complete. Four high-priority findings remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. A source close to the supervisor said some findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation found the closure evidence insufficient. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The evidence does not support picking a side. CL-05 is the Group's own board minutes, which record what management told the Board. It is not independent verification. EX-07 is a press report that relies on an unnamed source close to the supervisor, and the Group declined to comment. Neither source reports a primary independent validation. PV-09 says the client deferred independent validation of closure, and PV-05 says the sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred. CL-08 says screening tuning was last reviewed in 2023, and CL-09 lists sanctions tuning and remediation closure validation as FY2026 audits. These facts are consistent with the press account but do not prove it. CL-01 (FY2025) is more cautious than CL-05 and says some workstreams will extend into the next financial year. CL-10 uses the softer wording 'well advanced and moving to completion'. EX-03 and EX-02 show that closure claimed by management assertion is often unreliable. That is context, not proof about Meridian. Recency does not settle it, because the dates of the press report relative to the board minutes are not established. The conflict should be reported as unresolved, with the lack of independent validation flagged as a key exposure.
