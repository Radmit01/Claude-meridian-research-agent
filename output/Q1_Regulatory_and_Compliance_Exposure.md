# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Financial Crime, AML and Sanctions

- Board identifies financial crime risk (AML and sanctions compliance) as a principal risk, particularly in Institutional Services where onboarding volumes have grown sharply. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Growth in Institutional Services (fund administration and depositary, Luxembourg and Singapore) is expected to increase onboarding volumes of complex corporate and fund structures by about 140 per cent, increasing financial crime exposure. [CL-04, Four Strategic Pillars]
- Payments and sanctions screening is performed via a third-party vendor solution and tuning was last reviewed in 2023, a gap against the supervisory emphasis on periodic tuning and testing. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at client request and carried forward, so it is unassured in the year-one scope. Protiviti's access to second-line financial crime was limited because that scope was retained in-house. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Second line headcount was 214 against an approved establishment of 247; financial crime has the largest vacancy gap with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Supervisors expect CDD, EDD for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice, with periodic tuning, adequate resourcing, and control capability scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- A regulator thematic review of 14 fund/institutional services firms found beneficial ownership identification inadequate at nine, sanctions screening untuned for 24 months at eight, and financial crime resourcing lagging onboarding growth at eleven. Sector-wide; it does not name Meridian. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- The FY2026 audit plan covers CDD, EDD, sanctions screening effectiveness and tuning, transaction monitoring, and closure validation of the remediation programme. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]

### Financial Crime Remediation Status (Unresolved Conflict)

- The Board was told all twelve high-priority supervisory review findings were remediated and closed, with closure evidence accepted by second line; three medium-priority findings remain open and no high-priority findings were open at the meeting date. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- Per persons familiar with supervisory dialogue, supervisors do not regard the programme as complete; four high-priority findings from the 2024 review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. Some findings reported closed were reportedly reopened after independent validation. Sourced to unnamed individuals; Meridian declined to comment. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The conflict is UNRESOLVED. CL-05 is management-reported and EX-07 relies on unnamed sources. No independent validation result or supervisor document is in the corpus. Circumstantial evidence leans toward EX-07 but does not settle it: CL-01 says workstreams extend into next year, PV-09 says independent validation was deferred pending completion, and CL-08 says tuning was last reviewed in 2023. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Management itself states certain financial crime workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- Independent validation of financial crime remediation closure was under consideration but deferred pending completion of the programme, implying remediation was not complete in 2025. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- The Board approved an increase in the Institutional Services onboarding volume appetite explicitly on the basis that financial crime controls were remediated and assessed effective, so the decision depends on the disputed claim. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- A peer faced enforcement after telling its board remediation was complete when independent testing found findings closed without adequate evidence. The supervisor treated inaccurate board reporting as the most serious aggravating factor, delaying corrective action by about eighteen months. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Analysts noted that, if confirmed, the discrepancy would echo the recent peer enforcement action where inaccurate board reporting aggravated the outcome. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Meridian's investor presentation described its regulatory remediation agenda as 'well advanced and moving to completion'. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]

### Operational Resilience and Third-Party Risk

- Important business services are identified but impact tolerances are still being set; mapping of third-party dependencies for the four most critical services is incomplete. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Incomplete dependency mapping is the most commonly incomplete element of operational resilience and is treated as a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Supplier B (fund administration) and Supplier C (payments and screening) have no documented exit plan; only Supplier A (core hosting) has one, against the regulatory expectation of exit plans for all critical suppliers. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Supplier C's contract lacks a right-to-audit clause; audit rights and incident notification provisions are inconsistent across the supplier estate. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- The Board requested a third-party remediation plan by end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Third-party exit planning for critical suppliers was scoped but not commissioned by Protiviti; dependency mapping was to be delivered internally first. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- A roughly 31-hour client servicing platform disruption in Q4 2025 affected retail and institutional clients; Meridian attributed it to a third-party hosting provider failure and said it reported the incident to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The estate has 312 applications, 47 business critical, fragmented by three acquisitions, with duplicate CRM platforms across the UK and Luxembourg; the legacy core banking platform is due to migrate in 2027. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- Digital operational resilience requirements extend to critical ICT providers: contractual minimum provisions, incident reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]

### Cyber, Cloud, Data Protection and Technology Change

- Privileged access review coverage is incomplete in the Asian entities; vulnerability remediation service levels were missed in each of the last four quarters; logging and monitoring does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted; security baselines are not consistently applied across regional entities, and IAM is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Internal audit found security baselines applied inconsistently with the Asian entities materially behind, weak vulnerability remediation discipline, and fragmented IAM with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Data protection and privacy risk is associated with migration of customer data to cloud platforms across multiple jurisdictions. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- The core platform modernisation is about four months behind baseline, data platform migration wave one slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend is up GBP 14 million. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- Assurance over the modernisation programme found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of status. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- The strategy plans full cloud migration of core banking, client servicing and data platforms by end 2028, decommissioning fourteen legacy applications, a transformation and resilience exposure. [CL-04, Technology Roadmap Summary]

### Conduct and Consumer Outcomes

- A GBP 31 million provision was recognised for the Retail Banking legacy product governance review; the final redress population is not yet determined, so the liability may increase. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Conduct was only an emerging risk in FY2024 and is a principal risk in FY2025. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including legacy products no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Outcomes-based obligations are extending into legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectation that firms identify affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

### AI Governance

- The Group has no approved AI governance framework, AI inventory, or model risk controls covering generative AI; management acknowledges these as prerequisites to deployment beyond pilot scale. [CL-04, Artificial Intelligence Ambition]
- The AI governance forum referenced in the Group strategy has not yet been constituted. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- There is no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling, short of the supervisory expectation of an AI system inventory. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The AI governance readiness review is deferred pending the framework, so there is no assurance over AI governance yet. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- The Group intends to deploy AI in onboarding document triage, adviser knowledge assistance, and internal audit and risk analytics. [CL-04, Artificial Intelligence Ambition]
- Supervisors say firms should not scale AI deployment ahead of governance; firms deploying AI in onboarding, credit or advice face the highest expectations. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]

### Governance, Capacity and Strategic Dependencies

- The Group CRO position has been vacant since November 2025, with Deputy CRO Hannah Okafor as interim cover; no permanent appointment is in place. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The FY2025 effectiveness review identified inconsistent regional risk reporting, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and no defined AI governance framework. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- The pace of regulatory change is a material demand on second and third line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- The FY2026 plan needs about 6,200 audit days against internal capacity of 4,900, a shortfall of about 1,300 days, to be met through co-source in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- The 2026-2028 strategy depends on completion of the financial crime remediation portfolio, group-wide operational resilience capability, and recruitment into the second line vacancy gap. [CL-04, Dependencies and Enablers]
- Management flagged that failure to complete remediation would constrain regulatory approval for expansion of the Luxembourg and Singapore entities. [CL-04, Dependencies and Enablers]
- Firms that pursue growth in regulated activity before completing control remediation are materially more likely to experience supervisory intervention. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- The analyst expects regulatory overhang on financial crime to constrain inorganic options and approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]

### Financial Impact

- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, attributed principally to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment and the GBP 31m redress provision; cost-to-income rose to 71.2% from 67.8% and CET1 fell to 14.1% from 14.9%. [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- Mid-size firms face a structural squeeze between rising compliance cost and subscale distribution. [EX-08, Thesis]

### Recommended Engagement Focus Areas

- Prioritise independent validation of financial crime remediation closure (twelve high-priority findings). The management-vs-press conflict is unresolved and only independent testing can settle it. The Board's appetite increase and the expansion approvals depend on it, and a peer was penalised for inaccurate board reporting. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Perform the deferred sanctions screening tuning audit. Tuning was last reviewed in 2023 and the audit is unassured. EX-07 names tuning among open findings. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Assess whether the Institutional Services onboarding appetite increase was approved on reliable evidence and whether control capacity (19 vacant financial crime roles) supports the planned ~140% growth in complex onboarding. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Commission exit planning for Suppliers B and C and a contractual audit-rights review. Exit planning was scoped but not commissioned, and the Board's Q1 2026 remediation plan deadline has passed. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Support completion of impact tolerances and dependency mapping for the four most critical services, given that supervisors treat incomplete mapping as a material shortcoming and a 31-hour third-party outage occurred. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Offer AI governance framework and inventory design before AI is scaled. No framework, inventory, forum or assurance exists, and onboarding triage deployment is planned. [CL-04, Artificial Intelligence Ambition]
- Resolve the open co-source scope question, including extension into financial crime disciplines, to address the ~1,300 audit day shortfall. [PV-09, Open Items at Time of Writing]
- Scope work to determine the affected customer population for legacy product redress, since the GBP 31m provision may increase. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of Meridian's financial crime remediation programme (are all high-priority supervisory findings closed?)

- **Source A:** The Board was told the programme is substantially complete. All twelve high-priority supervisory review findings were remediated and closed, with closure evidence accepted by second line. Only three medium-priority findings remain open. No high-priority financial crime findings were open at the meeting date. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Per persons familiar with supervisory dialogue, supervisors do not regard the programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. Some findings previously reported closed were reopened after independent validation found insufficient closure evidence. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The evidence does not let me pick a side. CL-05 is Meridian's own management-reported position, recorded in board minutes. EX-07 is a press report that relies on unnamed sources, and Meridian declined to comment. Neither has independent primary evidence of the supervisor's actual position. Other documents lean toward EX-07 without settling it. CL-01 says some workstreams extend into the next year. PV-09 says independent validation of closure was deferred pending completion. PV-05 says Protiviti had limited access to second-line financial crime and the sanctions tuning audit was deferred. CL-08 says tuning was last reviewed in 2023. EX-02 and EX-03 show that closure by management assertion is a known failure pattern. These are circumstantial and some are not Meridian-specific. No independent validation result or supervisor document is in the corpus, so the conflict is reported as unresolved. The board's reliance on the claim for the Institutional Services appetite increase (CL-05 Item 7) makes it material.
