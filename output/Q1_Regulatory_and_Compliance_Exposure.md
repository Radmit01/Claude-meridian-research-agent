# Research Pack: What are Meridian Financial Group's principal regulatory and compliance exposures?

## 1. Findings

### Financial crime and remediation status

- Management says substantial progress has been made on the financial crime remediation portfolio, but certain workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year (i.e. not complete). [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- Position A: Q3 2025 board minutes record the programme as 'substantially complete'. All twelve high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review are said to be remediated and closed with second-line acceptance. Three medium-priority findings are open and due for closure within the quarter. The Head of Compliance confirmed no high-priority findings remain open, and the board asked for the programme to be prepared for formal closure. This is management's own assertion. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- Position B (unverified): a press report citing persons familiar with the supervisory dialogue says supervisors do not regard the remediation as complete. It says four high-priority findings remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. It also says findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation found insufficient closure evidence. The sources are unnamed and the Group declined to comment on supervisory correspondence. The evidence does not allow either position to be preferred. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Investors were told the regulatory remediation agenda is 'well advanced and moving to completion'. This is softer than 'substantially complete' and is possibly in tension with the open-findings reports. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- The board approved an increase in the onboarding volume appetite threshold in Institutional Services, on the basis that financial crime controls had been remediated and were assessed as effective. Exposure increases if the remediation claim is inaccurate. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Independent validation of financial crime remediation closure was deferred by the client pending completion of the programme. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Protiviti had limited access to the second line financial crime team because that scope was retained in-house. This is an assurance gap on financial crime. [PV-05, Relationship Observations]
- Sanctions screening is performed through a third-party vendor solution, and its tuning was last reviewed in 2023. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at client request and carried forward, so sanctions screening effectiveness is not independently assured in FY2024. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]
- Four FY2025 audits were carried into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- The 38-audit plan covers financial crime (including sanctions tuning and closure validation of the remediation programme), operational resilience (including third-party dependency mapping), third-party risk, technology and cyber, conduct and redress, and data and privacy (cross-border transfers, retention, regulatory reporting data quality). [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Analysts questioned management on the regulatory position on financial crime controls and on the sustainability of cost reduction targets given programme delays. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Second line headcount was 214 against an approved establishment of 247. The financial crime function has the largest vacancy gap, with 19 unfilled roles. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Group Head of Compliance Martin Ashby is accountable for the financial crime remediation portfolio. New CEO Alanna Reyes (appointed Feb 2026) has a mandate including completion of the regulatory remediation agenda. [CL-03, Executive Committee]

### Supervisory expectations and sector/peer enforcement read-across

- Supervisors expect CDD, EDD for complex structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice. They stress periodic tuning and testing and adequate resourcing of the financial crime function. Control capability must be scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at nine of fourteen reviewed firms. Firms relied on client attestation without independent verification beyond the second ownership layer. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Sanctions screening configurations had not been tuned or independently tested within 24 months at eight firms. Fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked documented rationale. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Financial crime resourcing had not kept pace with onboarding growth at eleven firms. Several firms accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Closure of remediation findings was in several cases evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report the outcome to their boards. Where growth depends on onboarding more complex structures, firms should evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider was subject to enforcement over financial crime control failings across a four-year period. It received a financial penalty and had to appoint an independent skilled person. [EX-03, Summary]
- The peer told its board remediation was complete, but independent testing found a number of findings closed without adequate evidence. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor called inaccurate internal reporting to the board the most serious aggravating factor. It delayed corrective action by about eighteen months. Boards must satisfy themselves on the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider the read-across, specifically on remediation closure assurance and alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Analysts noted that, if confirmed, the discrepancy would echo a recent peer enforcement action in which inaccurate internal board reporting was an aggravating factor. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Firms that pursue growth in regulated activity before completing control remediation are materially more likely to experience supervisory intervention. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- Supervisors are moving toward demonstrable effectiveness testing over framework documentation, expecting independent validation of control performance and of remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- Regulatory overhang on financial crime is likely to constrain Meridian's inorganic options and approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Meridian's institutional services growth ambition concentrates the Group in the activity attracting the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]

### Operational resilience and third-party risk

- Important business services have been identified, but impact tolerances are still being set. Mapping of third-party dependencies for the four most critical services was incomplete, and a further update was requested. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Firms must have identified important business services, set impact tolerances, mapped supporting resources including third parties, and completed scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is treated as a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Meridian had a client platform disruption of about 31 hours in Q4 2025, attributed to a third-party hosting provider failure. It affected retail and institutional clients. The Group said no client data was compromised and the incident was reported to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Operational resilience dependency mapping support was not taken up. The client indicated internal delivery in the first instance. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- The board considered dependence on three critical technology suppliers. Exit plans exist for only one, and audit rights and incident notification provisions are inconsistent across suppliers. A remediation plan was requested by end Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]
- Supplier B (fund administration) and Supplier C (payments and screening) have no documented exit plan. Supplier C's contract also has no right-to-audit clause. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Firms are expected to keep a complete outsourcing register, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. This bears directly on Suppliers B and C in CL-08. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Third-party exit planning is absent for two of three critical suppliers. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Third-party exit planning for critical suppliers was scoped but not commissioned, consistent with the exit planning gap in PV-05. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT third-party providers: contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party contractual arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]

### Cyber, technology and data protection

- Privileged access review coverage is incomplete in the Asian entities. Vulnerability remediation service levels were missed in each of the last four quarters. Logging and monitoring does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Cloud security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities. About 34% of workloads are cloud-hosted. Identity and access management is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Security baselines are applied inconsistently across regional entities, with the Asian entities materially behind. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Weak discipline in vulnerability remediation, with service levels missed repeatedly. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Identity and access management is fragmented, with no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Cyber risk is a top sector theme, with credential compromise and third-party compromise the dominant incident vectors. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- There is increasing scrutiny of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and use of personal data in training and operating AI systems. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- Fourteen of fifteen planned audits were delivered. Audits covered cloud security (UK/Luxembourg), IAM, vulnerability management, third-party onboarding and monitoring, data protection and cross-border transfers, and modernisation change management phase one. [PV-05, Audits Delivered]
- The estate has 312 applications, 47 of them business critical. It is fragmented after three acquisitions, with duplicate client servicing and payments capability. The core banking migration is scheduled for 2027, and wave one of the data platform cloud migration is delayed to Q2 2026. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- The core platform programme is about four months behind baseline. Data platform migration wave one slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend is up GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- Programme assurance found optimistic milestone reporting and insufficient independent challenge of status in the modernisation programme. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]

### Conduct, consumer outcomes and redress

- A GBP 31m provision has been recognised for the legacy product governance review in Retail Banking. The review of affected customers is ongoing and the final redress population is not yet determined, so the ultimate liability is uncertain. [CL-06, Item 8 — Consumer Outcomes]
- Management said the legacy redress population remains under review and a further provision cannot be ruled out. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including legacy products no longer sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Conduct and legacy product remediation is a top sector theme, with redress provisions frequently revised upwards after initial recognition. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend into legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectation on identifying affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

### Artificial intelligence governance

- The AI governance forum referenced in the Group strategy has not yet been constituted, and the FY2025 effectiveness review found no defined framework for governing artificial intelligence. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- There is no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling across the Group. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The AI governance readiness review is deferred pending establishment of the framework. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- AI governance framework design was discussed only at an exploratory level with the CTO, so there is no committed AI governance work. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Firms deploying AI must keep an inventory of AI systems, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors say firms should not scale deployment ahead of establishing governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Emerging AI requirements converge on: AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency to affected individuals, bias and drift testing, and senior-management accountability. Firms deploying AI in onboarding, credit or advice face the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Most multi-year core migrations report schedule slippage. AI deployment is materially outpacing control frameworks. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]

### Governance, capacity and board-identified principal risks

- The board identifies principal risks as financial crime (AML/sanctions, especially Institutional Services where onboarding grew sharply), operational resilience (concentration on few critical third-party tech providers), conduct in Retail Banking (legacy product governance weaknesses), data protection/privacy (cloud migration across jurisdictions), technology change (core platform modernisation), and emerging AI risk with the governance framework still under development. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- FY2024 principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology and cyber, and regulatory change risk. Conduct risk was only an emerging risk and AI was not a discrete risk category, indicating conduct and AI exposures have escalated by FY2025. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- The Group is authorised and regulated by the FCA and PRA, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. During FY2025 it engaged with supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness and consumer outcomes. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Management states the pace of regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk, AI governance) is a material demand on second and third line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Regulated entities include Meridian Financial Services (Ireland) DAC, Meridian Fund Services S.A. (Luxembourg), Meridian Asia Pte Ltd (Singapore) and Meridian Capital Markets (HK) Limited. [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- The Group CRO position has been vacant since November 2025, with interim cover by Deputy CRO Hannah Okafor. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- The board discussed the forthcoming CRO departure, agreed an external search and approved interim arrangements. [CL-05, Item 9 — Chief Risk Officer Succession]
- The Committee's FY2025 effectiveness review identified three gaps: inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and absence of a defined AI governance framework. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- The Board has eleven directors (six independent NEDs). The Audit Committee met seven times and the Risk Committee six. There are three lines of defence, with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and historically co-sources technology, cyber and financial crime audit work. The Audit Committee asked management to review the sustainability of this arrangement given the expanded FY2026 plan. [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- The plan needs about 6,200 audit days against an internal capacity of 4,900. The shortfall of about 1,300 days is to be met through co-sourcing, mainly in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- The client had not confirmed whether the FY2026 co-source would be extended, expanded to include financial crime, or re-tendered. [PV-09, Open Items at Time of Writing]
- Second line risk and compliance roles are scarce, with financial crime function vacancy rates at historic highs. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]

### Financial impact and strategic exposure

- Profit before tax fell to GBP 214m from GBP 241m, attributed principally to elevated regulatory remediation costs, technology investment, and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. Cost-to-income rose to 71.2% (67.8%) and CET1 fell to 14.1% (14.9%). [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- Management guided to elevated regulatory and remediation costs continuing through FY2026, moderating thereafter. [CL-10, Guidance]
- FY2024 announced a GBP 180m, four-year core platform modernisation and expansion of Institutional Services (fund administration and depositary in Luxembourg and Singapore), which later drive technology change and financial crime onboarding exposure. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- Meridian's 2028 efficiency target depends on delivering a technology programme that is already reporting slippage. Its cost-to-income ratio worsened year on year and is above the peer median. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Execution of the control agenda is the principal determinant of the equity story over the medium term. Firms typically increase spend on external assurance and specialist advisory support during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Mid-size firms are disproportionately affected by margin pressure, regulatory compliance cost and technology debt, because they lack the scale of larger institutions but carry comparable regulatory obligations. [EX-04, Macro Context]

### Recommended Engagement Focus Areas

- Prioritise obtaining independent evidence on whether the financial crime remediation is actually closed. CL-05 board minutes say all twelve high-priority findings are closed, while EX-07 reports four still open and some closures reopened after validation. The conflict is unresolved and the open-findings claim is unverified, so this is the central exposure to test. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Test the quality of closure evidence behind board assurances. EX-03 shows inaccurate board reporting was the most serious aggravating factor in a peer enforcement action, and EX-02 found closure by management assertion in several firms. If EX-07 is right, Meridian could face the same aggravating factor. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Seek access to the confidential annex of supervisory correspondence, or flag it as a limiting gap. It is the only evidence that could settle the remediation conflict and may hold other material regulatory exposures, but it is not reproduced in the corpus. [CL-06, Item 7 — Regulatory Correspondence]
- Examine whether the Institutional Services onboarding appetite increase was approved on a remediation claim that may be inaccurate. Combined with 19 unfilled financial crime roles, this is the growth-ahead-of-controls pattern supervisors link to intervention. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Assess the financial crime function's capacity. It has the largest second line vacancy gap, 19 roles of 33 across the second line, and EX-02 found resourcing lagging onboarding growth at eleven firms. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- Focus on sanctions screening tuning. It was last reviewed in 2023, the audit was deferred in FY2024 and again into FY2026, and EX-07 lists it among the open findings. EX-02 notes that a 24-month gap without independent tuning is a supervisory finding. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Treat financial crime assurance as an independence gap. Protiviti had limited access to the second line financial crime team, and independent validation of closure was deferred by the client, so no independent view exists. [PV-05, Relationship Observations]
- Concentrate on third-party exposure. Suppliers B and C lack exit plans and Supplier C lacks audit rights, which EX-01 treats as expected controls. The Q4 2025 31-hour outage from a third-party hosting failure shows the risk has already materialised. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Prioritise completion of important business services, impact tolerances and dependency mapping. The board noted incomplete mapping for the four most critical services, and EX-01 treats this as a material shortcoming. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Raise AI governance as an emerging exposure. No framework, forum or model inventory exists, the readiness audit is deferred, and no AI work has been committed. EX-01 says firms should not scale AI ahead of governance. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- Examine the legacy redress exposure. The GBP 31m provision is provisional, the affected population is undetermined, management has not ruled out a further provision, and EX-04 says such provisions are frequently revised upwards. [CL-10, Analyst Question and Answer Summary]
- Review the technology control gaps in the Asian entities. Privileged access coverage is incomplete, vulnerability SLAs were missed for four quarters, and logging coverage is partial. PV-05 independently reports the same pattern. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- Consider the effect of the vacant Group CRO role during the remediation period. Interim cover by the Deputy CRO coincides with the open remediation, a new CEO and capacity shortfalls. [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- Assess whether Internal Audit can deliver the FY2026 plan, including closure validation of the remediation. The plan needs about 6,200 days against 4,900 available, and the shortfall is to be co-sourced. [CL-09, Resourcing]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of Meridian's financial crime remediation programme: whether all high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review are closed

- **Source A:** Q3 2025 board minutes record the programme as 'substantially complete'. All twelve high-priority findings are said to be remediated and closed with second-line acceptance, and three medium-priority findings are open. The Head of Compliance confirmed no high-priority findings remain open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** A press report citing persons familiar with the supervisory dialogue says supervisors do not regard the remediation as complete. It says four high-priority findings remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. It also says findings reported as closed were reopened after independent validation found insufficient closure evidence. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The evidence does not let us pick a side. CL-05 is a primary internal record, but it reflects management's own assertion. EX-07 is secondary and rests on unnamed sources, and the Group declined to comment. The supervisory correspondence that would settle it is in a confidential annex (CL-06 Item 7) and is not reproduced anywhere. Other documents do not settle it either. CL-01 says some workstreams extend into the next financial year. CL-10 says 'well advanced and moving to completion', which is softer than 'substantially complete'. EX-02 and EX-03 show that closure by management assertion is a known failure pattern, and PV-09 says independent validation of closure was deferred. Those points make EX-07 plausible but do not prove it. Independent validation is planned in the CL-09 audit plan, but no result is available. Both positions should be reported, with the open-findings claim flagged as unverified.
