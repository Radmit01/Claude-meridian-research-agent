### External Regulatory Expectations and Sector Context

- Firms must demonstrate that CDD, EDD for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring are effective in practice, not merely well designed. Supervisors place emphasis on periodic tuning and testing of screening and monitoring, and on the adequacy of financial crime function resourcing. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Where firms expand into higher-risk client segments, control capability must be demonstrably scaled in advance of growth. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Firms must have identified their important business services, set impact tolerances, mapped supporting resources (including third parties) and completed scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most commonly incomplete element and is considered a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to maintain a complete register of material outsourcing arrangements, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products that are no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Firms deploying AI or generative AI are expected to keep an AI system inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors indicate that firms should not scale deployment ahead of establishing governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at nine of the fourteen firms reviewed. Firms relied on client attestation, without independent verification beyond the second ownership layer. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- EDD triggers were applied inconsistently, especially where a client relationship spanned multiple entities or jurisdictions within a group. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At eight firms, sanctions screening configurations had not been tuned or independently tested within the prior 24 months. Fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked a documented rationale. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rule sets were largely inherited from legacy implementations and had not been recalibrated for changes in client mix. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At eleven firms, financial crime resourcing had not kept pace with onboarding growth. Several firms accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report to their boards. Where growth depends on onboarding more complex structures, firms should evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider faced enforcement action for financial crime failings over a four-year period, resulting in a financial penalty and a requirement to appoint an independent skilled person. [EX-03, Summary]
- The firm expanded its fund services without commensurate financial crime resourcing. CDD files for complex structures were materially incomplete, and sanctions screening had gone three years without an independent tuning review. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor described inaccurate internal reporting to the board as the most serious aggravating factor, noting that it delayed corrective action by about eighteen months. Boards must satisfy themselves on the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider read-across, specifically in relation to remediation closure assurance and the alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- The sector faces margin pressure, high compliance cost and technology debt, with mid-size firms disproportionately affected. [EX-04, Macro Context]
- Key risk themes include: financial crime control effectiveness (growth into complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions); operational resilience and concentration on critical providers; cyber risk (credential and third-party compromise); conduct and legacy product remediation (with redress often revised upward); technology transformation slippage; AI governance lagging deployment; and talent scarcity, with historically high financial crime vacancy rates. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Firms pursuing growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to face supervisory intervention. The sequencing of strategy and control investment is a differentiator. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- Mid-size financial services firms face a structural squeeze between rising compliance cost and subscale distribution, with continued consolidation expected over the next 36 months. [EX-08, Thesis]
- Financial crime regulatory overhang is likely to constrain inorganic options and the approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Execution of the control agenda is the principal determinant of the equity story. Firms in this position typically increase spend on external assurance and specialist advisory support during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Emerging requirements converge on the following: an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. AI used in onboarding, credit or advice faces the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT providers, including contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- Scrutiny is increasing of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in AI training and operation. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- The direction of travel is towards demonstrable effectiveness testing rather than framework documentation, with supervisors expecting independent validation of control performance and remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend into legacy and closed-book products, with a heightened expectation that firms identify affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of the financial crime remediation programme: high-priority findings closed or still open

- **Source A:** The Board was told the programme is substantially complete. All twelve high-priority findings were remediated and closed with second-line-accepted evidence. Three medium-priority findings remain open, and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Unnamed sources familiar with the supervisory dialogue say four high-priority findings from the 2024 review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. They also say some findings previously reported closed were reopened after independent validation because the closure evidence was insufficient. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** Neither source can be preferred on the evidence. CL-05 is an authoritative primary record, but it reflects what management told the Board, and EX-03 and EX-02 show that closure by management assertion has been unreliable in this sector. EX-07 is independent of management, but it relies on anonymous sources and is explicitly unconfirmed. The two accounts also differ in what they count: CL-05 reports twelve high-priority findings closed, while EX-07 reports four open and some reopened. CL-01 ('substantial progress', with workstreams extending into next year) and CL-10 ('well advanced and moving to completion') are both more cautious than CL-05 and do not settle the question. Settling it needs independent closure validation or the supervisor's own position, and the corpus contains neither. Flag as unresolved.

**Subject:** Progress of the core platform modernisation programme and the cost-to-income target

- **Source A:** Investors are told that modernisation is on track to deliver a 62% cost-to-income ratio by 2028. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- **Source B:** The Board minutes record the programme as about four months behind baseline. Data platform wave one has slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend is up GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- **Resolution basis:** authority/independence
- **Resolution:** The internal Board minutes are the more authoritative source on actual programme status. The market-facing messaging in CL-10 is promotional. Other sources support the slippage: CL-08 (wave one delayed to Q2 2026), EX-08 (programme 'already reporting slippage') and PV-05 (optimistic milestone reporting). Treat the programme as delayed, with the 'on track' investor message inconsistent with internal reporting. The 62% target itself is not shown to be unachievable.
