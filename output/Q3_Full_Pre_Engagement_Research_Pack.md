# Research Pack: Produce the full pre-engagement client research pack for Meridian Financial Group.

## 1. Findings

### Corporate profile, strategy, financial position and governance

- Meridian Financial Group plc is a mid-size financial services group headquartered in London. It operates in the UK, Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong, employs approximately 4,800 people, and had assets under administration of GBP 91.4bn at 31 December 2025. [CL-01, Section 1 — Group Overview]
- The group has three segments: Wealth & Asset Management (46% of revenue), Retail Banking (34%) and Institutional Services (20%). [CL-01, Section 1 — Group Overview]
- FY2025 total income was GBP 1,284m (FY2024: GBP 1,196m). Profit before tax was GBP 214m (FY2024: GBP 241m). The cost-to-income ratio was 71.2% (FY2024: 67.8%), the CET1 ratio was 14.1% (FY2024: 14.9%), and customer accounts totalled 1.9m (FY2024: 1.7m). The fall in profit before tax was driven by remediation costs, technology investment and a GBP 31m legacy customer redress provision in Retail Banking. [CL-01, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- At 31 December 2024, assets under administration were GBP 83.7bn, and the group had approximately 4,400 staff across five jurisdictions. The segment mix was Wealth & Asset Management 49%, Retail Banking 33% and Institutional Services 18%. [CL-02, Section 1 — Group Overview]
- FY2024 figures were: total income GBP 1,196m, profit before tax GBP 241m, cost-to-income ratio 67.8%, CET1 ratio 14.9% and 1.7m customer accounts. These form the baseline against which the FY2025 deterioration is measured. [CL-02, Section 2 — Financial Highlights]
- The jurisdictions referenced (UK, Ireland, Luxembourg and Asia) indicate Meridian's regional footprint. [PV-01, Differentiators Presented]
- Meridian's stated ambition is to be a leading mid-size wealth and institutional services provider, operating on a single modern technology estate with a materially lower cost-to-income ratio. [CL-04, Strategic Ambition]
- The strategy rests on four pillars. (1) Grow Institutional Services: double fund administration and depositary revenue by 2028, in Luxembourg and Singapore, with onboarding volumes of complex structures up approximately 140%. (2) Modernise technology: move all core, client servicing and data platforms to the cloud by the end of 2028 and decommission 14 legacy applications. (3) Improve efficiency: reduce the cost-to-income ratio to 62% by 2028 and consolidate into two hubs. (4) Embed responsible AI, subject to a group AI governance framework. [CL-04, Four Strategic Pillars]
- In FY2024, Meridian announced a multi-year modernisation of its core banking and client servicing platforms, with indicative investment of GBP 180m over four years. It also announced Institutional Services expansion through fund administration and depositary services in Luxembourg and Singapore. [CL-02, Section 4 — Strategic Commentary]
- The Board has eleven directors, six of whom are independent non-executive directors. The Audit Committee met seven times and the Risk Committee six times. The group operates a three lines of defence model, with Internal Audit reporting functionally to the Audit Committee Chair. [CL-02, Section 5 — Governance]
- The other executives are: CFO Peter Vance (since 2021); COO Devan Mistry (accountable for core platform modernisation); CTO Sofia Lindqvist (appointed 2024); Group Head of Compliance Martin Ashby (accountable for financial crime remediation); and Chief Internal Auditor Rosalind Mbeki (since 2019). [CL-03, Executive Committee]
- Second line headcount is 214 against an approved establishment of 247, leaving 33 vacancies. The financial crime function has the largest gap, with 19 unfilled roles. The risk function comprises Enterprise Risk, Financial Crime Risk, Technology and Operational Risk, and Compliance Advisory. [CL-03, Second Line Structure]
- The regional entities are Meridian Financial Services (Ireland) DAC (Dublin), Meridian Fund Services S.A. (Luxembourg), Meridian Asia Pte Ltd (Singapore) and Meridian Capital Markets (HK) Limited (Hong Kong). [CL-03, Regional Entities]
- The Risk Committee has five members, four of whom are independent non-executive directors, and meets at least six times a year. It oversees the risk framework, risk appetite, principal risks (financial crime, operational resilience, technology/cyber, conduct and compliance), the adequacy of resourcing, and emerging risks including AI. The CRO attends; the Deputy CRO attends in the absence of a permanent appointment. [CL-07, Composition and Meetings]
- The Committee is supported by four executive forums: the Group Risk Management Committee, the Financial Crime Committee, the Technology and Operational Resilience Committee, and the Conduct and Customer Outcomes Forum. The AI governance forum referenced in the strategy has not yet been constituted. [CL-07, Governance Structure Beneath the Committee]
- Meridian's FY2025 cost-to-income ratio of 71.2% compares with a peer median of 66.4% and an upper quartile of 61%. It is above the peer median and has deteriorated year on year. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Meridian's CET1 ratio of 14.1% compares with a peer median of 15.2% and an upper quartile of 16.8%, placing it below the peer median. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Regulatory and remediation spend is 4.8% of income, against a peer median of 2.9% and an upper quartile of 1.9%, which is materially elevated relative to peers. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Second line headcount is 4.5% of total headcount, against a peer median of 6.1% (upper quartile 7.4%), indicating understaffing relative to peers. The financial crime function's vacancy rate is 19%, against a peer median of 11% (upper quartile 6%), placing it in the highest quartile of vacancy. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Meridian's cost-to-income ratio has deteriorated year on year and is above the peer median. The stated 2028 efficiency target depends on sustained delivery of a technology programme that is already reporting slippage. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Meridian presents itself as a focused mid-size wealth and institutional services group. It sees a path to margin expansion through technology simplification and operational efficiency. [CL-10, Equity Story]
- Institutional Services is the growth engine, with a target to double revenue by 2028. Technology modernisation is said to be on track to deliver a 62% cost-to-income ratio by 2028. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]

- The principal risks identified by the Board are: financial crime (especially given Institutional Services onboarding growth); operational resilience (third-party concentration); conduct and customer outcomes in Retail Banking; data protection arising from cloud migration; technology change arising from core platform modernisation; and emerging AI risk, with governance still under development. [CL-01, Section 3 — Principal Risks and Uncertainties]
- The FY2024 principal risks were credit, market, operational, financial crime, technology and cyber, and regulatory change. Conduct was only an emerging risk and AI was not a discrete category; both had been elevated by FY2025. [CL-02, Section 3 — Principal Risks]
- The FY2025 effectiveness review identified three gaps: inconsistent risk reporting across regional entities, limited aggregation of third-party risk data, and the absence of a defined AI governance framework. [CL-07, Known Gaps]
- Important business services have been identified, but impact tolerances are still being set. Third-party dependency mapping for the four most critical services is incomplete, and the Board has requested a further update. [CL-05, Item 8 — Operational Resilience]
- Meridian depends on three critical technology suppliers, and exit plans exist for only one. Audit rights and incident notification provisions are inconsistent. The Board requested a remediation plan by the end of Q1 2026. [CL-06, Item 5 — Third-Party Risk]

- The technology estate is hybrid, with about 34% of workloads cloud-hosted. Cloud security baselines are defined but not consistently applied across regional entities. Identity and access management is federated in the UK but separate in Singapore and Hong Kong. [CL-08, Cloud Posture]
- Supplier A (core hosting/infrastructure) has an exit plan. Supplier B (fund administration platform) has no documented exit plan. Supplier C (payments and screening) has no documented exit plan, and its contract contains no right-to-audit clause. [CL-08, Critical Third-Party Suppliers]
- Privileged access review coverage is incomplete in the Asian entities. Vulnerability remediation SLAs were missed in each of the last four quarters. Logging and monitoring does not cover all business-critical applications. [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- There is no consolidated inventory of models or AI-enabled tooling across the Group. This is a gap against the regulator's expectation of an AI inventory (EX-01). [CL-08, Known Technology Control Gaps]
- The Group runs 312 applications, 47 of which are business critical. The estate is materially fragmented as a result of three historic acquisitions, with duplicate core capability in client servicing and payments. [CL-08, Estate Summary]
- Core banking runs on a legacy, on-premise, vendor-supported platform scheduled for migration in 2027. Two CRM/client servicing platforms run in parallel across the UK and Luxembourg entities. The fund administration platform is vendor-hosted by a third party in Luxembourg. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- Payments and sanctions screening run through a third-party vendor solution, and tuning was last reviewed in 2023. [CL-08, Core Platforms]
- In Q4 2025, Meridian suffered a disruption of approximately 31 hours to client servicing platforms, affecting a segment of retail and institutional clients. The disruption was attributed to a failure at a third-party hosting provider. No client data was compromised, and the incident was reported to supervisors. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The FY2026 internal audit plan is risk-based and comprises 38 audits. It is informed by principal risks, supervisory priorities, prior audit findings and the change portfolio. [CL-09, Planning Basis]
- Planned coverage includes financial crime (CDD, EDD, sanctions screening tuning, transaction monitoring, and closure validation of the remediation programme), operational resilience, third-party risk, technology and cyber, change/core modernisation assurance, conduct, and data and privacy. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Group Internal Audit has 41 practitioners and has historically co-sourced specialist technology, cyber and financial crime audit work. The Audit Committee asked management to review the sustainability of the co-source arrangement given the expanded FY2026 plan (relevant to the Protiviti opportunity). [CL-03, Third Line Structure]
- The FY2026 plan expands coverage of financial crime, operational resilience, third-party management, cloud security and AI governance. Full delivery requires additional specialist resource beyond the approved establishment, and a co-source arrangement is being considered. [CL-06, Item 6 — Internal Audit Plan FY2026]
- The plan requires about 6,200 audit days against internal capacity of 4,900. The shortfall of roughly 1,300 days is to be met through co-source arrangements, mainly in cyber, cloud, financial crime and resilience. This represents a Protiviti opportunity. [CL-09, Resourcing]
- Four FY2025 audits were deferred into FY2026: sanctions screening tuning, Hong Kong entity governance, model risk management, and third-party exit planning. [CL-09, Prior Year Carry-Forward]
- The AI governance readiness review has been deferred pending establishment of the framework. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- Audit plan days delivered against planned stand at 92%, compared with a peer median of 97% and an upper quartile of 100%, indicating slight underdelivery. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Only 33% of critical suppliers have a documented exit plan (peer median 71%, upper quartile 95%), and only 40% of important business services have completed dependency mapping (peer median 78%, upper quartile 100%). Both represent significant gaps relative to peers. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Meridian's cloud workload share is 34%, compared with a peer median of 52% and an upper quartile of 71%. Meridian is behind peers on cloud migration. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Meridian scores 0% on the approved AI governance framework metric (peer median 45%, upper quartile 100%). No framework is in place. [EX-05, Peer Benchmarking]
- Third-party exit planning was absent for two of three critical suppliers. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]
- Identity and access management is fragmented, and there is no single view of privileged access. [PV-05, Principal Themes Reported]

### Financial crime remediation status and conflict

- Management states that substantial progress has been made on the financial crime remediation portfolio, but certain workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year (i.e. the programme is not complete). This is relevant to the conflict between the CL-05 account and external reporting. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- The Q3 2025 Board was told the programme is "substantially complete": all twelve high-priority supervisory findings were remediated and closed with second-line-accepted evidence; three medium-priority findings remain open and are due for closure in the quarter; and no high-priority findings are open. The Board was asked to prepare for formal closure and to move the remaining items to business as usual (BAU). This conflicts with external reporting that findings remain open (per project notes, EX-07). [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- The Board approved an increased onboarding volume appetite threshold in Institutional Services, premised on financial crime controls having been remediated and assessed as effective. This decision depends on the contested remediation claim. [CL-05, Item 7 — Risk Appetite Review]
- Persons familiar with the supervisory dialogue say that supervisors do not regard the financial crime remediation programme as complete. They report that four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification for complex fund structures and sanctions screening tuning. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- This contrasts with Meridian's Q3 2025 board reporting, which described the programme as substantially complete, with all high-priority findings closed. A person close to the supervisor said that some findings previously reported as closed were reopened after independent validation testing because the closure evidence was insufficient. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The Group declined to comment on supervisory correspondence, and its investor presentation described the regulatory remediation agenda as "well advanced and moving to completion". The claims about supervisory views come from unnamed sources and are unconfirmed. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- The regulatory remediation agenda is described to investors as "well advanced and moving to completion". This is relevant to the conflict over whether financial crime remediation is complete. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- The plan includes closure validation of the financial crime remediation programme, which implies that closure had not yet been independently validated. [CL-09, Thematic Coverage]
- In several cases, closure of remediation findings was evidenced by management assertion rather than independent validation. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- The peer reported to its board that remediation was complete, but independent testing later found that findings had been closed without adequate evidence. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor called inaccurate internal reporting to the board the most serious aggravating factor, as it delayed corrective action by about 18 months. Boards must satisfy themselves on the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider the read-across, particularly on remediation closure assurance and on aligning growth strategy with control capacity. This is directly relevant to Meridian's "remediation substantially complete" board claim versus the external report of open findings. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- Analysts noted that the discrepancy, if confirmed, would echo a recent enforcement action against a peer firm in which inaccurate internal reporting to the board was an aggravating factor. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- Supervisors are moving toward demonstrable effectiveness testing over framework documentation, and they expect independent validation of control performance and remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- A key failure mode is growth being approved on the basis of a remediation programme reported as complete when closure evidence has not been independently validated. This is directly relevant to Meridian, given the disputed status of its financial crime remediation. [PV-08, Three Failure Modes]
- Protiviti treats closure validation as a distinct assurance activity: closure evidence is tested independently against the original finding, and a finding is closed only where the underlying control is demonstrated to operate effectively, not on management assertion. This is relevant to the disputed completeness of Meridian's financial crime remediation. [PV-04, Closure Validation Principle]
- Independent validation of financial crime remediation closure was under consideration by the client but was deferred pending completion of the programme. [PV-09, Areas Discussed with the Client]
- Meridian kept the financial crime audit in-house rather than co-sourcing it to Protiviti. This is relevant to the independence of assurance over the area of greatest supervisory concern. [PV-01, Outcome]
- Protiviti's internal audit work had limited visibility of financial crime second-line activity, so it cannot independently speak to the remediation status. The sanctions screening tuning audit was deferred at the client's request and carried forward, which is a coverage gap in the audit of financial crime controls. [PV-05, Deferred Audit]

### Regulatory expectations, enforcement trends and external/peer context

- Meridian is authorised by the FCA and PRA, with subsidiary authorisations in Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore and Hong Kong. In FY2025 it engaged supervisors on financial crime controls, operational resilience readiness and consumer outcomes. Management says that regulatory change (operational resilience, digital operational risk, AI governance) is a material demand on second- and third-line capacity. [CL-01, Section 4 — Regulatory Environment]
- Supervisors expect customer due diligence (CDD), enhanced due diligence (EDD) for complex ownership structures, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice, not only in design. They stress periodic tuning and testing, resourcing of the financial crime function, and control capability scaled ahead of growth into higher-risk segments. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Firms must have identified important business services, set impact tolerances, mapped supporting resources (including third parties), and completed scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most commonly incomplete element and is regarded as a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to maintain a complete register of material outsourcing, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products that are no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Firms deploying AI must maintain an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors say firms should not scale deployment ahead of governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- The review covered financial crime control frameworks at fourteen firms providing fund administration, depositary and institutional services, with a focus on the onboarding of complex corporate and fund structures. [EX-02, Scope]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at nine of fourteen firms, which relied on client attestation. EDD triggers were applied inconsistently across multi-entity or multi-jurisdiction relationships. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At eight firms, sanctions screening had not been tuned or independently tested in the preceding 24 months, and fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked a documented rationale. Meridian's screening tuning was last reviewed in 2023 (CL-08). [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rules were largely inherited from legacy implementations and had not been recalibrated for changes in client mix. Financial crime resourcing lagged onboarding growth at eleven firms, and several accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report the outcome to their boards. Firms whose growth depends on onboarding complex structures should show that control capability was scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- Emerging AI requirements converge on an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. AI used in onboarding, credit or advice attracts the highest expectations. This is relevant given that Meridian has no approved AI governance framework. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT providers: contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- There is increasing scrutiny of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in AI training and operation. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend to legacy and closed-book products, with a heightened expectation that firms identify affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider was subject to enforcement for financial crime failings over a four-year period. It received a financial penalty and accepted a requirement to appoint an independent skilled person. [EX-03, Summary]
- The peer expanded fund services without commensurate financial crime resourcing. CDD files for complex structures were incomplete in a material proportion of the sample. Sanctions screening had no independent tuning review for three years. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The sector faces margin pressure, high compliance costs and technology debt arising from consolidation. Mid-size firms are disproportionately affected. [EX-04, Macro Context]
- The top risk themes are: financial crime control effectiveness for complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions; operational resilience and provider concentration; cyber risk (credential and third-party compromise); conduct and legacy redress (often revised upwards); core migration slippage; AI governance lagging deployment; and scarcity of financial crime talent. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Firms that grow regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to face supervisory intervention. The sequencing of strategy and control investment is emerging as a differentiator of supervisory outcome. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- Mid-size financial services firms face a structural squeeze between rising compliance cost and subscale distribution. Continued consolidation is expected over the next 36 months. [EX-08, Thesis]
- Financial crime regulatory overhang is likely to constrain inorganic options and the approval of entity expansion. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- The institutional services growth ambition is sound in principle, but it concentrates the Group in the activity attracting the greatest supervisory scrutiny. [EX-08, Implications for Meridian]
- Execution of the control agenda is the principal determinant of the equity story. Firms in this position typically increase spend on external assurance and specialist advisory support during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Supervisor correspondence received in Q4 2025 is recorded in a confidential annex to the minutes and is not reproduced. Its content is unknown, which leaves a gap in the picture of regulatory expectations and enforcement. [CL-06, Item 7 — Regulatory Correspondence]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Whether the financial crime remediation programme is complete and all high-priority findings are closed

- **Source A:** The Q3 2025 board minutes say the programme is 'substantially complete'. All twelve high-priority supervisory findings are described as remediated and closed with second-line-accepted evidence, and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** The press report cites unnamed people familiar with the supervisory dialogue. They say supervisors do not regard the programme as complete, and that four high-priority findings from the 2024 review remain open. These include beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** The board minutes are an authoritative first-party record, but they report management's own assertion. The press report is independent of Meridian but relies on anonymous sources and is explicitly unconfirmed, and Meridian declined to comment. The internal audit plan includes closure validation of the programme, which suggests closure has not been independently validated. Peer enforcement material also shows management closure claims have proved unreliable. Together these point toward the external account but do not prove it. Neither source can be preferred on recency or authority, so the dispute stays open pending independent validation.

**Subject:** Whether financial crime remediation work is finished or still in delivery

- **Source A:** The Q3 2025 board was told the programme is substantially complete, was asked to prepare for formal closure, and was told remaining items would move to business-as-usual. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** The FY2025 annual report says substantial progress has been made but certain workstreams remain in delivery and will extend into the next financial year, so the programme is not complete. [CL-01, Section 5 — Statement on Remediation Activity]
- **Resolution basis:** recency
- **Resolution:** The annual report covers the full year to December 2025 and was published after the Q3 2025 board meeting. It is therefore the more recent Meridian statement, and it is more cautious. It is consistent with the investor messaging that remediation is 'well advanced and moving to completion', which is not the same as complete. The later, more conservative position is that work continues into the next year. The gap between 'substantially complete' and 'extending into next year' is partly a matter of wording, so the resolution is moderate-confidence.
