### Regulatory Expectations, Peer Enforcement and Sector Context

- The regulator expects customer due diligence (CDD), enhanced due diligence (EDD) for complex ownership, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring to be effective in practice. It places emphasis on periodic tuning and testing of screening and monitoring, and on the adequacy of financial crime function resourcing. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Where firms expand into higher-risk client segments, supervisors expect control capability to be demonstrably scaled in advance of growth. [EX-01, Priority Area One — Financial Crime Controls]
- Firms must identify important business services, set impact tolerances, map supporting resources including third parties, and complete scenario testing. Incomplete dependency mapping is the most commonly incomplete element and is considered a material shortcoming. [EX-01, Priority Area Two — Operational Resilience]
- Firms are expected to maintain a complete register of material outsourcing, documented exit plans for all critical suppliers, contractual audit rights, and a concentration risk assessment. [EX-01, Priority Area Three — Third-Party and Outsourcing Risk]
- Firms must evidence good customer outcomes across the product lifecycle, including for legacy products that are no longer actively sold. [EX-01, Priority Area Four — Consumer Outcomes]
- Multi-jurisdiction firms must evidence lawful transfer mechanisms, defined retention schedules, and control over data used in cloud environments. [EX-01, Priority Area Five — Data and Cross-Border Transfers]
- Firms deploying AI/GenAI are expected to maintain an AI inventory, apply proportionate governance and model risk controls, evidence human oversight of material decisions, and assess consumer and conduct implications. Supervisors say firms should not scale deployment ahead of governance. [EX-01, Priority Area Six — Artificial Intelligence]
- Beneficial ownership identification for multi-layered fund structures was inadequate at nine of the fourteen firms reviewed. Firms relied on client attestation without independent verification beyond the second layer. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Enhanced due diligence (EDD) triggers were applied inconsistently, particularly where a client relationship spanned multiple entities or jurisdictions within a group. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At eight firms, sanctions screening had not been tuned or independently tested in the preceding 24 months, and fuzzy matching thresholds often lacked documented rationale. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Transaction monitoring rule sets were largely inherited from legacy implementations and had not been recalibrated for changes in client mix. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- At eleven firms, financial crime resourcing had not kept pace with onboarding growth, and several firms accepted growth targets without a control capacity plan. [EX-02, Principal Findings]
- Firms should self-assess against the findings and report to their boards. Where growth depends on onboarding more complex structures, firms should evidence that control capability has been scaled in advance. [EX-02, Supervisory Expectation]
- A comparable mid-size institutional services provider was penalised and required to appoint an independent skilled person in respect of financial crime failings over a four-year period. [EX-03, Summary]
- The peer firm expanded its fund services without commensurate financial crime resourcing, and its CDD files for complex structures were materially incomplete. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The firm told its board that remediation was complete, but independent testing found that findings had been closed without adequate evidence. Sanctions screening had also gone three years without an independent tuning review. [EX-03, Nature of the Failings]
- The supervisor regarded inaccurate internal reporting to the board as the most serious aggravating factor, noting that it delayed corrective action by about eighteen months. It stated that boards must satisfy themselves on the quality of closure evidence rather than rely on management assertion. [EX-03, Regulatory Commentary]
- Supervisors have written to sector firms asking them to consider read-across, specifically in relation to remediation closure assurance and the alignment of growth strategy with control capacity. [EX-03, Read-Across]
- The sector faces margin pressure, high compliance costs and technology debt, with mid-size firms disproportionately affected. [EX-04, Macro Context]
- Key themes are: financial crime control effectiveness for complex structures and higher-risk jurisdictions; operational resilience and critical provider concentration; cyber risk (credential and third-party compromise); conduct and legacy product remediation, with redress often revised upward; technology transformation slippage; AI governance lagging deployment; and talent scarcity, with financial crime vacancy rates at historic highs. [EX-04, Top Risk Themes]
- Firms that pursue growth in regulated activity ahead of completing control remediation are materially more likely to face supervisory intervention; the sequencing of strategy and control investment is a differentiator. [EX-04, Observation on Sequencing]
- Mid-size financial services firms face a structural squeeze between rising compliance costs and subscale distribution, and continued consolidation is expected over the next 36 months. [EX-08, Thesis]
- Execution of the control agenda is regarded as the principal determinant of the medium-term equity story. Firms in this position typically increase spend on external assurance and specialist advisory support during remediation and validation. [EX-08, View]
- Emerging AI requirements converge on the following: an AI system inventory, use-case risk classification, human oversight of consequential decisions, transparency to affected individuals, bias and drift testing, and senior management accountability. AI used in onboarding, credit or advice faces the highest expectations. [EX-09, Artificial Intelligence]
- Requirements extend to critical ICT third-party providers and include contractual minimum provisions, incident classification and reporting timelines, threat-led penetration testing, and a register of information covering all third-party arrangements. [EX-09, Digital Operational Resilience]
- Scrutiny is increasing of cross-border transfer mechanisms, retention discipline, and the use of personal data in AI training and operation. [EX-09, Data and Privacy]
- There is a movement towards demonstrable effectiveness testing over framework documentation, with supervisors expecting independent validation of control performance and remediation closure. [EX-09, Financial Crime]
- Outcomes-based obligations continue to extend into legacy and closed-book products, with heightened expectations around identifying affected customer populations. [EX-09, Consumer and Conduct]

## 2. Conflicts and Reconciliation

**Subject:** Status of the financial crime remediation programme (complete vs. high-priority findings still open)

- **Source A:** The Board was told the programme is 'substantially complete'. All twelve high-priority findings were remediated and closed, with closure evidence accepted by the second line. Three medium-priority findings remain open, and no high-priority findings are open. [CL-05, Item 6 — Financial Crime Remediation Programme]
- **Source B:** Unnamed sources familiar with the supervisory dialogue say supervisors do not regard the programme as complete. Four high-priority findings from the 2024 supervisory review remain open, including beneficial ownership verification and sanctions screening tuning. Some findings previously reported closed were reopened after independent validation found the closure evidence insufficient. [EX-07, Remediation Status Questioned]
- **Resolution basis:** unresolved
- **Resolution:** Neither side can be preferred on the evidence. CL-05 is a primary Board record, but it rests on management assertion, and the closure evidence is not reproduced (CL-05 extract covers only Items 6–9). EX-07 is a secondary press report relying on unnamed sources and is explicitly unconfirmed. CL-01 ('substantial progress', workstreams extending into next year) and CL-10 ('well advanced and moving to completion') are softer than CL-05 but do not settle the point. EX-02 and EX-03 show closure by management assertion is a known failure mode. That makes the discrepancy credible without proving it. Only independent closure validation or the supervisor's own position would settle it. The risk-appetite increase in CL-05 Item 7 depends on this claim.

**Subject:** Progress of the technology modernisation programme (investor messaging vs. internal Board reporting)

- **Source A:** Investors were told technology modernisation is 'on track' for the 62% cost-to-income target by 2028. [CL-10, Key Messages to Market]
- **Source B:** Board minutes record the programme as about four months behind baseline. Data platform migration wave one has slipped to Q2 2026, and forecast spend has increased by GBP 14m. [CL-06, Item 4 — Core Platform Modernisation Programme]
- **Resolution basis:** authority/independence
- **Resolution:** CL-06 is the more reliable source. It is an internal governance record of programme status, and it is corroborated by independent sources. EX-08 describes a technology programme 'already reporting slippage'. CL-08 confirms the data platform wave one delay to Q2 2026. PV-05 found optimistic milestone reporting on the programme. CL-10 is promotional investor messaging. The Board minutes should therefore be preferred, and the 'on track' message treated as inconsistent with them. A caveat is that CL-10 may rely on a different baseline, and no source explains the difference.
